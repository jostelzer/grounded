# Synthesis — What can someone with stage 2 hypertension do to lower blood pressure without medication, and how many mmHg is each move actually worth?

## Verdict
Non-drug measures reliably lower blood pressure, and the arithmetic is knowable: cutting sodium, changing the whole dietary pattern, losing weight, training, and — for heavy drinkers — cutting alcohol each buy a few millimetres of mercury, and stacking them buys more than any one alone. Under supervised trial conditions a combined programme moves clinic systolic pressure by roughly ten to sixteen mmHg, which is the same order as a blood-pressure drug; under ordinary conditions, where people shop and cook for themselves, the same programmes deliver closer to four. That gap, not the biology, is the binding constraint. The effects are also larger for people whose pressure starts high, which is exactly the stage 2 situation, but the evidence base was built mostly in people with milder elevation, and the strongest single lever with hard outcome evidence — a potassium-enriched salt substitute — was tested in an older, higher-risk rural Chinese population. Lifestyle change is first-line therapy that also makes drugs work better; at stage 2 it is a complement to a medical decision, not a substitute for one.

## Throughline
Every non-drug lever has a credible number attached to it, and the gap between the number measured under supervision and the number delivered in ordinary life is the real story.

## Claims

### C1. Lifestyle modification is the first-line treatment for hypertension, its components are partially additive, and it increases the effect of drug therapy rather than replacing it.
- strength: strong — consensus review in a major general journal, drawing on the randomised record
- evidence: narrative review of hypertension treatment; states that first-line therapy is lifestyle modification (weight loss, low-sodium/high-potassium dietary pattern, physical activity, moderation or elimination of alcohol), that component effects are partially additive and enhance the efficacy of pharmacologic therapy, and that the decision to start medication rests on the level of blood pressure and atherosclerotic cardiovascular risk [@Carey2022treatment]
- contrary: none found — searched
- boundary: a review statement, not a trial result; it describes what guidelines recommend, not what any individual will achieve
- depends-on: —
- numbers: hypertension defined as systolic ≥130 mmHg or diastolic ≥80 mmHg; ~116 million US adults, >1 billion worldwide; a 10 mmHg systolic reduction lowers cardiovascular event risk by approximately 20–30%; only 44% of US adults with hypertension are controlled below 140/90
- actors: Robert M Carey, Andrew E Moran, Paul K Whelton (Carey et al. 2022)

### C2. Cutting dietary sodium lowers blood pressure in a dose-dependent way, and lowers it more in people whose starting pressure is higher.
- strength: strong — 133 randomised trials with sodium confirmed by 24-hour urine collection
- evidence: systematic review and meta-analysis, 133 trials, 12,197 participants; a mean reduction of 130 mmol/day of urinary sodium lowered systolic pressure 4.26 mmHg (95% CI 3.62 to 4.89) and diastolic 2.07 mmHg (1.67 to 2.48); each 50 mmol cut 1.10 mmHg systolic (0.66 to 1.54); systolic falls were larger in older people, in non-white populations, and at higher baseline systolic pressure [@Huang2020effect]
- contrary: in unadjusted meta-regression there was no association between the size of the sodium cut and the size of the blood-pressure fall; the dose-response emerges only after adjusting for duration, age, sex, ethnicity and baseline pressure [@Huang2020effect]
- boundary: trials shorter than 15 days underestimate the effect (1.05 vs 2.13 mmHg per 50 mmol); the estimates are for achieved sodium change measured in urine, not for advice given
- depends-on: —
- numbers: 133 trials, 12,197 participants; 130 mmol/day → −4.26 (3.62–4.89) systolic, −2.07 (1.67–2.48) diastolic; 50 mmol → −1.10 (0.66–1.54) systolic, −0.33 (0.04–0.63) diastolic; short trials −1.05 (0.40–1.70) vs longer −2.13 (0.85–3.40) mmHg per 50 mmol
- actors: Liping Huang, Kathy Trieu, Bruce Neal, Feng J. He (Huang et al. 2020)

### C3. Changing the whole dietary pattern lowers blood pressure on top of cutting salt, and several patterns work rather than one.
- strength: strong — a controlled feeding trial plus a 67-trial network meta-analysis
- evidence: DASH-Sodium feeding trial, 412 participants, each sodium level eaten for 30 days; the DASH diet lowered systolic pressure at every sodium level, and within the control diet, moving from high to intermediate sodium cut 2.1 mmHg and intermediate to low a further 4.6 mmHg, versus 1.3 and 1.7 mmHg on DASH [@Sacks2001effects]; network meta-analysis of 67 trials, 13 dietary approaches, 17,230 hypertensive and pre-hypertensive participants, in which DASH, Mediterranean, low-carbohydrate, Palaeolithic, high-protein, low-glycaemic-index, low-sodium and low-fat patterns all beat control for systolic pressure [@Schwingshackl2018comparative]
- contrary: the diet and the salt cut are partly redundant — the benefit of cutting sodium was smaller for people already eating the DASH pattern, so the two do not simply add [@Sacks2001effects]
- boundary: DASH-Sodium supplied every meal; a 2026 trial testing home-delivered DASH-patterned groceries with dietitian counselling in Black Boston residents with systolic 120 to under 150 mmHg is the community-setting test [@Juraschek2026dash]
- depends-on: C2
- numbers: 412 participants, 30 days per sodium level; control diet −2.1 then −4.6 mmHg systolic; DASH diet −1.3 then −1.7 mmHg; network meta-analysis 67 trials, 17,230 participants, effective patterns down to −8.73 mmHg systolic
- actors: —

### C4. Regular exercise lowers resting blood pressure, with isometric training ranking highest among modalities.
- strength: moderate — very large randomised base, but ranking rests on indirect network comparison and the trials are mostly small
- evidence: pairwise and network meta-analysis of 270 randomised trials, 15,827 participants; aerobic training −4.49/−2.53 mmHg, dynamic resistance −4.55/−3.04, combined −6.04/−2.54, high-intensity interval training −4.08/−2.50, isometric training −8.24/−4.00; isometric ranked first on SUCRA (98.3%) [@Edwards2023exercise]; pooled isometric estimates across meta-analyses run 5–9 mmHg systolic and 1–4 diastolic [@Edwards2024isometric]
- contrary: in adults with blood pressure ≥130/85 the isometric effect on office pressure (−6.97 mmHg, 95% CI −8.77 to −5.18) did not carry over to 24-hour ambulatory systolic pressure (−2.77, −6.80 to 1.25), and the certainty of that evidence is very low to low with high heterogeneity [@Hansford2021effectiveness]; head-to-head trials against aerobic training conflict, trial quality is mid-range with unblinded assessors and weak placebo control, and some studies show pressure returning toward baseline within about ten days of stopping [@Edwards2024isometric]
- boundary: isometric safety evidence covers healthy, pre-hypertensive and stage 1 populations, not obesity, diabetes or complex disease; caution is advised in coronary artery disease because of abrupt diastolic falls [@Edwards2024isometric]; no randomised trial has compared exercise directly against blood-pressure drugs, and only 56 of 197 exercise trials enrolled hypertensive participants (3,508 people) [@Naci2018does]
- depends-on: —
- numbers: 270 trials, 15,827 participants; aerobic −4.49/−2.53; dynamic resistance −4.55/−3.04; combined −6.04/−2.54; HIIT −4.08/−2.50; isometric −8.24/−4.00 mmHg; isometric office −6.97 (−8.77 to −5.18) vs 24-h ambulatory −2.77 (−6.80 to 1.25); 391 trials in the drug comparison (197 exercise, 10,461 participants; 194 drug, 29,281)
- actors: Jamie Edwards, Jonathan D. Wiles, Jamie M. O'Driscoll (Edwards et al. 2023)

### C5. Losing weight lowers blood pressure in proportion to the weight lost, at roughly one mmHg systolic per kilogram.
- strength: strong — 25 randomised trials, consistent dose relationship
- evidence: meta-analysis of 25 randomised trials, 4,874 participants; a net 5.1 kg loss (95% CI 4.25 to 6.03) lowered systolic pressure 4.44 mmHg (2.95 to 5.93) and diastolic 3.57 (2.25 to 4.88); per kilogram, 1.05 mmHg systolic (0.66 to 1.43) and 0.92 diastolic (0.55 to 1.28) [@Neter2003influence]
- contrary: none found — searched
- boundary: reductions were significantly larger where average weight loss exceeded 5 kg; the estimate is for weight actually lost, which is the hard part
- depends-on: —
- numbers: 25 trials, 4,874 participants; −5.1 kg → −4.44/−3.57 mmHg; per kg −1.05 (0.66–1.43) systolic; >5 kg loss −6.63 (4.82–8.43) vs ≤5 kg −2.70 (0.81–4.59) mmHg systolic
- actors: Judith E. Neter, Johanna M. Geleijnse (Neter et al. 2003)

### C6. Cutting alcohol lowers blood pressure only above a threshold of about two drinks a day, and most in the heaviest drinkers.
- strength: strong — 36 trials with a clear dose gradient
- evidence: systematic review and meta-analysis, 36 trials, 2,865 participants; in people drinking two or fewer drinks a day a reduction produced no significant blood-pressure fall, while in those drinking six or more a day who halved their intake systolic pressure fell 5.50 mmHg (95% CI 4.30 to 6.70) and diastolic 3.97 (3.25 to 4.70) [@Roerecke2017effect]
- contrary: none found — searched
- boundary: 2,464 of the 2,865 participants were men; the threshold means the lever is worthless to light drinkers
- depends-on: —
- numbers: 36 trials, 2,865 participants (2,464 men, 401 women); ≤2 drinks/day: no significant effect; ≥6 drinks/day halved: −5.50 (4.30–6.70) systolic, −3.97 (3.25–4.70) diastolic
- actors: Michael Roerecke, Jürgen Rehm (Roerecke et al. 2017)

### C7. Combining the levers under supervision produces reductions of about 10 to 16 mmHg systolic, the range of a blood-pressure drug.
- strength: moderate — several randomised trials agree on the magnitude, but each is small and the network evidence carries low certainty
- evidence: ENCORE trial, 144 unmedicated overweight adults with systolic 130–159 or diastolic 85–99; clinic pressure fell 16.1/9.9 mmHg on DASH plus weight management, 11.2/7.5 on DASH alone and 3.4/3.8 in controls, with ambulatory pressure following the same pattern [@Blumenthal2010effects]; TRIUMPH trial, 140 patients with resistant hypertension, four months of supervised diet and exercise cut clinic systolic 12.5 mmHg (95% CI 10.2 to 14.9) versus 7.1 (3.7 to 10.4) after a single education session, and 24-hour ambulatory systolic 7.0 versus 0.3 [@Blumenthal2021effects]; network meta-analysis of 54 trials and 5,827 patients ranked combinations far above single interventions [@Chen2024updated]
- contrary: the network meta-analysis's headline combination estimates — lifestyle plus tai chi at 21.75 mmHg systolic (95% CI 10.02 to 33.25), breathing exercise at 17.00 (7.72 to 26.28) — rest on few small trials with very wide intervals, and CINeMA rated confidence very low to moderate across most comparisons [@Chen2024updated]
- boundary: ENCORE and TRIUMPH delivered supervised, centre-based programmes with dietitians and exercise staff; TRIUMPH participants were already on multiple drugs, so its effect is additive to medication, not a replacement for it
- depends-on: C1, C2, C3, C4, C5
- numbers: ENCORE n=144, −16.1/−9.9 vs −11.2/−7.5 vs −3.4/−3.8 mmHg; TRIUMPH n=140, clinic systolic −12.5 (10.2–14.9) vs −7.1 (3.7–10.4), ambulatory −7.0 vs −0.3; Chen network 54 trials, 5,827 patients, 21 interventions
- actors: James A. Blumenthal (Blumenthal et al. 2010; Blumenthal et al. 2021)

### C8. Outside supervised settings the same programmes deliver roughly a third of that, and what survives at four years is smaller again.
- strength: strong — large randomised trials in free-living participants
- evidence: PREMIER trial, 810 unmedicated adults with above-optimal pressure through stage 1 hypertension (120–159/80–95); after subtracting the advice-only group the net systolic reduction at six months was 3.7 mmHg for the established behavioural programme and 4.3 mmHg with DASH added [@Group2003effects]; Look AHEAD, 5,145 overweight or obese adults with type 2 diabetes, averaged over four years the intensive lifestyle arm lost 6.15% of body weight versus 0.88% and lowered systolic pressure 5.33 versus 2.97 mmHg [@Group2010long]
- contrary: self-monitoring of blood pressure recovers some of the gap when it is attached to real support — a 25-trial individual-patient meta-analysis found no effect from monitoring alone (−1.0 mmHg, 95% CI −3.3 to 1.2) but −6.1 mmHg (−9.0 to −3.2) when combined with intensive support, and the largest effects were in people with higher baseline systolic pressure up to 170 mmHg [@Tucker2017self]
- boundary: PREMIER excluded people on antihypertensive drugs and enrolled mostly stage 1 hypertension; Look AHEAD participants all had type 2 diabetes
- depends-on: C7
- numbers: PREMIER n=810, net systolic −3.7 and −4.3 mmHg at 6 months; Look AHEAD n=5,145, 4-year averages: weight −6.15% vs −0.88%, systolic −5.33 vs −2.97, diastolic −2.92 vs −2.48 mmHg; self-monitoring −3.2 mmHg (1.6–4.9) overall, −1.0 (−3.3 to 1.2) alone, −6.1 (3.2–9.0) with intensive support
- actors: —

### C9. Replacing table salt with a potassium-enriched substitute is the only non-drug measure with randomised evidence of fewer strokes and deaths.
- strength: strong for the tested population — a 21,000-person cluster-randomised trial with hard endpoints, supported by meta-analysis
- evidence: SSaSS, 600 villages in rural China, 20,995 people with a history of stroke or aged 60+ with high blood pressure, salt of 75% sodium chloride and 25% potassium chloride versus regular salt over a mean 4.74 years; stroke rate ratio 0.86 (95% CI 0.77 to 0.96), major cardiovascular events 0.87 (0.80 to 0.94), death from any cause 0.88 (0.82 to 0.95), with no significant excess of serious hyperkalaemia (rate ratio 1.04, 0.80 to 1.37) [@Neal2021effect]
- contrary: the meta-analysis pooling long-term salt-substitution trials found that seven of the eight trials reporting outcomes were done in China or Taiwan, three in residential facilities, and seven in older or higher-risk populations, so generalisability outside that setting is uncertain [@Greenwood2024long]
- boundary: potassium substitutes are not for people with advanced kidney disease or on potassium-retaining drugs; the trial population was older and higher-risk than a typical newly diagnosed stage 2 patient
- depends-on: C2
- numbers: 20,995 people, 600 villages, 4.74 years mean follow-up; stroke 29.14 vs 33.65 per 1000 person-years (RR 0.86, 0.77–0.96); MACE 49.09 vs 56.29 (0.87, 0.80–0.94); death 39.28 vs 44.61 (0.88, 0.82–0.95); hyperkalaemia serious adverse events 3.35 vs 3.30 (1.04, 0.80–1.37); Greenwood 16 RCTs, 8 with outcomes, mortality analysis 6 RCTs / 27,710 participants
- actors: Bruce Neal, Yangfeng Wu, Maoyi Tian (Neal et al. 2021)

### C10. The evidence base was built largely in people with milder elevation than stage 2, so the honest reading is that lifestyle change complements a medical decision rather than settling it.
- strength: moderate — an inference from the enrolment criteria of the decisive trials, plus the consensus review
- evidence: PREMIER enrolled 120–159/80–95 mmHg and excluded medicated participants [@Group2003effects]; ENCORE enrolled 130–159/85–99 [@Blumenthal2010effects]; only 56 of 197 exercise trials enrolled participants above 140 mmHg [@Naci2018does]; the consensus review places the decision to start medication on the level of blood pressure and cardiovascular risk, with lifestyle as the first-line therapy that enhances drug efficacy [@Carey2022treatment]
- contrary: the effect of lifestyle change is not weaker at higher pressures — sodium reduction works better at higher baseline systolic pressure [@Huang2020effect], self-monitoring with support works better up to 170 mmHg [@Tucker2017self], and supervised diet and exercise still lowered pressure in resistant hypertension [@Blumenthal2021effects]
- boundary: this is a claim about the evidence base and the clinical decision, not about biology
- depends-on: C1, C7, C8
- numbers: PREMIER 120–159/80–95 mmHg; ENCORE 130–159/85–99 mmHg; 56/197 exercise trials hypertensive (3,508 of 10,461 participants)
- actors: —

## Patterns
- P1. The supervision gap (C7, C8, C10): the same interventions produce roughly 10–16 mmHg under supervision and 4–5 mmHg when people are left to run them, and the difference is delivery, not biology.
- P2. Higher pressure, bigger payoff (C2, C8, C4): sodium reduction, self-monitoring and combined programmes all work better at higher baseline pressure, which cuts against the intuition that lifestyle is only for mild cases.
- P3. Surrogate versus outcome (C4, C9): almost every lever is measured in mmHg, and only salt substitution has randomised evidence of fewer strokes and deaths.
- P4. Office pressure flatters the levers (C4, C7): where 24-hour ambulatory pressure was measured, the effect was smaller than the clinic reading, most starkly for isometric training.

## Open
- Whether combined lifestyle programmes lower blood pressure as much in people starting above 160/100 as in the stage 1 populations most trials enrolled — the settling study is a randomised trial of a supervised programme recruiting only stage 2, unmedicated participants with ambulatory endpoints.
- Whether the salt-substitute outcome benefit transfers outside older, higher-risk East Asian populations eating home-cooked food — the settling study is an outcome trial in a Western population where most sodium comes from processed food [@Greenwood2024long].
- Whether isometric training's office-pressure advantage survives ambulatory measurement in adequately powered, sham-controlled trials [@Hansford2021effectiveness; @Edwards2024isometric].
