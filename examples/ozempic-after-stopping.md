## What the body does when Ozempic stops

*The last injection is the start of another phase, as appetite and blood-sugar effects fade. Studies find substantial weight regain, but their averages leave room for people who keep part of the loss—and for unanswered questions about how.*

At the end of a large semaglutide trial, the injections stopped—and the researchers kept watching [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725). For more than a year, adults with overweight or obesity had received weekly semaglutide or a dummy injection, alongside regular counselling about food and physical activity [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725). A smaller group agreed to return after treatment finished, so investigators could record their weight, blood pressure and blood tests over the following year [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725). The scale began to tell a different story: much of the weight lost on the medicine came back [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725).

That follow-up, the STEP 1 extension, found that participants previously taking semaglutide regained about two-thirds of their earlier weight loss within a year [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725). Many improvements in blood sugar, blood pressure and other measures of health moved back toward their starting levels too [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725). Yet the average concealed a second finding: nearly half of those with a final weight measurement remained at least about five per cent lighter than before treatment [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725).

Benefits often fade after stopping, and substantial regain is common; complete regain is not inevitable [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725), [Rubino et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224). There is also a crucial distinction behind that answer: the trial used a weekly dose of 2.4 mg in people without diabetes [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725). Ozempic and the obesity medicine Wegovy contain the same active ingredient, semaglutide, but were developed for different purposes—type 2 diabetes and obesity management, respectively [Yang & Yang 2024](https://doi.org/10.2147/dddt.s470826). STEP 1 studied people without diabetes at the obesity dose, and STEP 4 did too; those participants differ from Ozempic users treated for diabetes [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725), [Rubino et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224).

### Much comes back, some stays off

The STEP 1 investigators followed 327 participants from selected trial sites who had completed semaglutide or placebo treatment [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725). Everyone in this follow-up stopped their assigned treatment [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725). Both the injections and the trial's lifestyle counselling ended together [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725). That matters: the rise in weight followed the loss of a treatment package, so the study leaves the drug's share of the effect uncertain [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725).

The former semaglutide group lost an average 17.3% during treatment and remained 5.6% below its starting weight a year after stopping ([Figure 1](#fig-weight-course)) [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725). For a clearly hypothetical 100 kg starting weight, those two percentages calculate to about 17 kg lost during treatment and about 6 kg still lost a year afterward. This arithmetic illustrates the group averages.

A smaller lasting loss can still matter for health [Rubino et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224). The STEP 4 paper cites guidance recommending sustained losses of about 5% to 15% to improve many conditions linked to overweight and obesity [Rubino et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224). In the STEP 1 follow-up, nearly half of the former semaglutide participants with a final measurement still met the lower end of that range [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725). Some had lost much more during treatment than others, and the average regain did not send everyone to the same endpoint [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725).

Looking further ahead is harder [West et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2025-085304). A review combining weight-management studies estimated that regain after newer medicines, semaglutide and tirzepatide, averaged a little under a kilogram a month [West et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2025-085304). Its model projected a return to starting weight around a year and a half after stopping [West et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2025-085304). Most of the studies followed patients for much shorter periods, so that date extends a model beyond the observed results [West et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2025-085304). The measured year after STEP 1 is firmer ground than a forecast of what must happen next [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725), [West et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2025-085304).

### How the medicine changes a meal

To understand the rising scale, start with what happens at a meal [Blundell et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.12932). Semaglutide mimics [GLP-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucagon-like_peptide-1), a hormone released from the intestine that helps regulate blood sugar and appetite [Blundell et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.12932), [Yang & Yang 2024](https://doi.org/10.2147/dddt.s470826). During treatment, people can feel less hungry, more satisfied and better able to control eating [Blundell et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.12932). Researchers have tested those changes using something more concrete than a report of easier dieting: how much food people actually eat when given the chance [Blundell et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.12932).

In one small experiment, 30 adults with obesity were assigned to weekly semaglutide and placebo in a randomly chosen order; 28 completed both periods [Blundell et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.12932). Each period lasted 12 weeks, and the semaglutide dose rose gradually to 1 mg [Blundell et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.12932). Because the same people experienced both treatments, investigators could compare their eating under the two conditions [Blundell et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.12932).

After a standard breakfast, participants were given lunch and dinner and told to eat until pleasantly full [Blundell et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.12932). An evening snack box extended the test beyond the main meals, offering sweet and savoury options that people could keep until midnight [Blundell et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.12932). Researchers measured consumption and found that the energy eaten across those freely chosen meals and snacks was about 24% lower on semaglutide ([Figure 2](#fig-meal-test)) [Blundell et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.12932). Nausea ratings were similar under both treatments, helping distinguish reduced appetite from simply feeling too sick to eat on the test day [Blundell et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.12932).

The stomach is part of the story, but a permanently slow stomach is too simple an explanation [Friedrichsen et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14280). In another trial, 72 adults received semaglutide raised to the obesity dose or placebo for 20 weeks [Friedrichsen et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14280). At the end, a standard breakfast included yoghurt containing paracetamol [Friedrichsen et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14280). Researchers measured absorption of that pain medicine as an indirect guide to how the meal left the stomach [Friedrichsen et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14280). Participants later ate as much lunch as they wanted [Friedrichsen et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14280). The semaglutide group ate about a third less, yet the indirect test found no evidence of delayed stomach emptying at that point [Friedrichsen et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14280). The test can miss delays, especially in solid food leaving the stomach [Friedrichsen et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14280). Even so, the pairing of lower food intake with this test result points to a fuller appetite story [Friedrichsen et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14280).

What happens when that appetite effect is removed? How often appetite returns, and exactly when, remains uncertain [Wang et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.70571). One small study followed a single group [Wang et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.70571). It followed 28 women with obesity through semaglutide treatment and 12 weeks afterward, reporting returning appetite in roughly 80% [Wang et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.70571).

The medicine itself leaves gradually [Yang & Yang 2024](https://doi.org/10.2147/dddt.s470826). Its half-life is about a week: roughly half the remaining drug is eliminated over that interval [Yang & Yang 2024](https://doi.org/10.2147/dddt.s470826). Stopping injections therefore allows drug levels to fall over weeks [Yang & Yang 2024](https://doi.org/10.2147/dddt.s470826). A review of weight-loss biology describes another pressure toward regain: after losing weight, people can feel hungrier and sometimes burn less energy than expected even allowing for their smaller size [Reiss et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/biom15030408). Those pressures form part of the background to stopping appetite treatment [Reiss et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/biom15030408).

### The support stayed, the injection changed

The STEP 1 result leaves a puzzle: how much of the regain came from stopping medication, and how much from ending the counselling? STEP 4 changed the design so the support continued on both sides [Rubino et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224). Adults without diabetes first spent 20 weeks taking semaglutide as the dose rose to 2.4 mg [Rubino et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224). Investigators then assigned 803 people who had reached that dose either to continue semaglutide or to switch to a matching dummy injection [Rubino et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224).

Over the next 48 weeks, those continuing the medicine lost another roughly 8% of their weight, while those switched to placebo gained about 7% [Rubino et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224). These changes were measured from the point of the switch, after the initial treatment loss ([Figure 3](#fig-continue-stop)) [Rubino et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224). The opposite directions emerged while both groups still received monthly professional counselling [Rubino et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224). That comparison supplies stronger evidence that removing semaglutide itself promotes regain, although it describes people who had already managed to stay on the target dose [Rubino et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224).

The continuing support was specific: a daily food-energy target about 500 calories below estimated needs, 150 minutes of activity a week, and records of diet and activity reviewed at counselling visits [Rubino et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224). Regain in the placebo group occurred within that programme, showing the limits of treating support as a guaranteed replacement for medication [Rubino et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224).

There was a different trade-off in digestive symptoms [Rubino et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224). New gastrointestinal events during the comparison period were reported by about four in ten people continuing semaglutide and about 26% of those switched to placebo [Rubino et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224). This result describes new events over many months; it does not say when someone's existing nausea, vomiting or other digestive symptoms disappeared after stopping [Rubino et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224). The STEP 1 follow-up did not systematically collect adverse events, leaving a gap precisely where readers might hope for a recovery calendar [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725). Here, “withdrawal” means stopping treatment; these studies did not test for an addiction-like withdrawal syndrome [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725), [Rubino et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224).

### Blood sugar has its own story

Weight is only one outcome of stopping a medicine developed for diabetes [Yang & Yang 2024](https://doi.org/10.2147/dddt.s470826). Semaglutide helps the body release insulin and reduces release of [glucagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucagon), another hormone that regulates blood sugar [Yang & Yang 2024](https://doi.org/10.2147/dddt.s470826). The stimulation of insulin release depends on glucose levels [Yang & Yang 2024](https://doi.org/10.2147/dddt.s470826). Removing those effects can worsen glucose control, making weight trials in people without diabetes an incomplete guide to what stopping Ozempic means [Tzang et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2025.103680).

A medicine shortage provided a window into that problem in South Korea [Choe et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3803/enm.2024.2150). Researchers examined records from adults with type 2 diabetes who had been taking dulaglutide, a related GLP-1 medicine, alongside metformin [Choe et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3803/enm.2024.2150). When dulaglutide became unavailable, some stopped without a replacement and many switched to other oral diabetes medicines [Choe et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3803/enm.2024.2150). The analysis included 69 people with usable blood tests before and about three months after the interruption [Choe et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3803/enm.2024.2150).

Their average [HbA1c](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glycated_hemoglobin) rose by about a percentage point, and fasting blood glucose rose too [Choe et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3803/enm.2024.2150). HbA1c measures longer-term glucose exposure [Nathan et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc08-0545). Researchers have compared it with average glucose over the preceding three months, giving it a broader view than a single blood-sugar reading [Nathan et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc08-0545). The shortage study was observational, used dulaglutide rather than Ozempic, and left out people who started insulin or lacked suitable tests, all of which narrow the result [Choe et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3803/enm.2024.2150).

A broader review pooled four studies reporting HbA1c in diabetes and found an average rise of roughly 0.7 percentage points after GLP-1 treatment ended [Tzang et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2025.103680). It compared results at the end of treatment with those at least 12 weeks afterward [Tzang et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2025.103680). The studies used different medicines and follow-up care and their results varied greatly [Tzang et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2025.103680). Their combined figure supports worsening glucose control, with the size of the change for Ozempic users still uncertain [Tzang et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2025.103680).

Other benefits can recede alongside glucose control [Kosiborod et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14890). Further analyses of the same STEP 4 participants found that improvements in blood pressure and several blood-fat measures deteriorated after the switch to placebo [Kosiborod et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14890). These measurements track risk factors [Kosiborod et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14890). How they translate into actual heart attacks after stopping remains an open question [Kosiborod et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14890). The body can move in several directions at once, with fewer new digestive events but less favourable weight and metabolic readings [Rubino et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224), [Kosiborod et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14890).

### The average has exceptions

Outside a trial, stopping can look less uniform [Gasoyan et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.24331). A study of nearly 8,000 adults in an Ohio and Florida health system followed people starting semaglutide or tirzepatide for obesity [Gasoyan et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.24331). Those who stopped lost less overall than those who continued, but their average weight trajectories were relatively stable after discontinuation during the available follow-up [Gasoyan et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.24331). That finding complicates the idea that every last injection starts the same steep climb [Gasoyan et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.24331).

Many patients used lower doses and lost less weight than people in the major trials, which could help explain the contrast [Gasoyan et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.24331). “Stopping” meant a prescription-supply gap longer than about three months, sometimes followed by another refill [Gasoyan et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.24331). A year counted from starting treatment left late stoppers with much shorter follow-up afterward [Gasoyan et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.24331). Changes in diet or activity were not measured well enough to explain the difference [Gasoyan et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.24331).

Partial retention also appeared in a much smaller study of women with obesity and [polycystic ovary syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polycystic_ovary_syndrome) [Jensterle et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fendo.2024.1366940). They took semaglutide for 16 weeks while already receiving metformin, then continued metformin and lifestyle support for two years [Jensterle et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fendo.2024.1366940). The researchers reported regain of about a third of the semaglutide-associated loss, leaving much of it retained [Jensterle et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fendo.2024.1366940). With only 25 women and no group stopping without metformin, the study cannot identify metformin as the reason [Jensterle et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fendo.2024.1366940).

Together with the retained losses in STEP 1, these findings leave room for several outcomes after stopping [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725), [Gasoyan et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.24331), [Jensterle et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fendo.2024.1366940). They have not established which combination of initial loss, continuing treatment and daily habits reliably predicts who will retain it [Gasoyan et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.24331), [Jensterle et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fendo.2024.1366940).

### Keeping the loss is a second experiment

One maintenance experiment began before anyone's final injection [Jensen et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102475). In Denmark, adults with obesity first completed an eight-week low-calorie programme using four meal-replacement products a day [Jensen et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102475). Researchers then assigned 195 participants to a year of supervised exercise, daily liraglutide—a different GLP-1 medicine—the combination, or placebo [Jensen et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102475). After that year, the active treatments ended and participants were invited back a year later [Jensen et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102475).

Exercise had meant vigorous indoor cycling followed by circuit training, with participants encouraged to attend group sessions twice weekly and exercise individually twice weekly [Jensen et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102475). When supervision ended, they returned the sports watches and heart-rate monitors used in the programme [Jensen et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102475). The follow-up asked what remained after that organised support, or the medicine, was removed [Jensen et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102475).

People previously assigned liraglutide alone regained about 10 kg during the off-treatment year, compared with about 4 kg after exercise alone [Jensen et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102475). Regain after the combined programme was about 7 kg, but its difference from liraglutide alone was uncertain [Jensen et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102475). Only 109 participants returned, and some differences failed a stricter check for chance findings across multiple comparisons [Jensen et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102475). Exercise remains a plausible part of maintenance, with those limits and the different medicine leaving a semaglutide exit strategy unproven [Jensen et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102475).

Current guidance reflects the stronger evidence for continuing effective treatment [Pedersen et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1503/cmaj.250502). The 2025 Canadian adult obesity guideline recommends long-term use of effective obesity medication alongside changes in daily habits to sustain weight loss and avoid regain and the loss of health benefits [Pedersen et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1503/cmaj.250502). The programmes behind that discussion involve repeated food and activity counselling, records reviewed with professionals, or supervised exercise [Rubino et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224), [Jensen et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102475).

Tapering remains a question awaiting a completed comparison [Yevusiak et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0354237). A published semaglutide trial protocol proposes testing a gradual reduction over 16 weeks against immediate stopping, but it has yet to report results [Yevusiak et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0354237). Reviews describe reducing doses or spacing injections as possible maintenance approaches with limited supporting evidence [Reiss et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/biom15030408). A reliable general schedule has not emerged from these sources [Reiss et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/biom15030408), [Yevusiak et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0354237).

The appetite and blood-sugar effects of semaglutide depend on treatment that eventually leaves the body [Yang & Yang 2024](https://doi.org/10.2147/dddt.s470826). Stopping commonly brings substantial weight regain, while some people retain part of the loss [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725). The open question is which forms of continuing care can keep more of those benefits when the injections end; the proposed tapering comparison explores one possible route [Reiss et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/biom15030408), [Yevusiak et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0354237).

**Scope and methods**

This narrative review covers human research on stopping semaglutide, with related GLP-1 medicines explicitly identified. Literature searches on 5 October 2026 used PubMed and citation searching, including studies that complicate the main regain finding. Crossref was used to verify references. Animal experiments and claims about a personal symptom schedule or the proportions of muscle and fat regained were excluded. The small appetite-return study was available only as an abstract. The tapering protocol supplies no completed outcome results [Yevusiak et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0354237).
