## When Ozempic stops, the body's old settings start to return

*Semaglutide leaves slowly, but its appetite, weight, and glucose effects usually do not last on their own. Most people regain at least some weight; blood sugar and other risk markers often drift back; nausea and other stomach effects usually ease.*

In a follow-up to the STEP 1 trial, 327 adults stepped off weekly semaglutide after 68 weeks. They had lost an average of 17.3% of their body weight. One year later, they had regained 11.6 percentage points - roughly two-thirds of that loss - yet they were still 5.6% lighter than when they began [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725). The graph did not show a trapdoor. It showed a slope.

That slope is the story of stopping Ozempic. Semaglutide is not known to produce a classic chemical withdrawal syndrome. Instead, the drug's effects fade and the biology it had been holding back becomes audible again: hunger, food reward, glucose production, and the body's persistent defense of its prior weight. One caveat matters. Ozempic is semaglutide prescribed for type 2 diabetes at lower doses than the 2.4 mg obesity regimen sold as Wegovy; most direct withdrawal evidence comes from the higher-dose obesity trials, while diabetes-specific stopping data are thinner [Sorli et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/s2213-8587%2817%2930013-x), [Ceriello et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41574-026-01258-5).

### The last injection keeps working for weeks

Semaglutide has a [biological half-life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_half-life) of about seven days. After the final dose, its concentration falls by roughly half each week, so meaningful exposure fades over about five weeks rather than ending the next morning [Hall et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40262-018-0668-z). There is no single day on which the body flips from "on Ozempic" to "off Ozempic."

While present, this [GLP-1 receptor agonist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GLP-1_receptor_agonist) makes eating easier to regulate. In controlled studies, semaglutide reduced hunger and food cravings, increased fullness, and cut freely chosen meal energy by about 24% to 39% [Blundell et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.12932), [Friedrichsen et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14280), [Gabe et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.15802), [Gibbons et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14255). A 60-week trial found that people taking 2.4 mg still ate about 240 to 292 fewer calories at laboratory meals than those taking placebo, even after some subjective appetite differences had faded [Tronieri et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajcnut.2026.101403). As drug exposure falls, that pharmacological brake weakens. Hunger or persistent thoughts about food can return, and maintaining the same intake may require more conscious effort.

### The scale usually turns, but not all the way at once

The most direct experiment was STEP 4. After a 20-week semaglutide run-in, 803 adults were randomized either to continue 2.4 mg or switch to placebo while both groups kept receiving lifestyle support. Over the next 48 weeks, continuers lost another 7.9%; switchers gained 6.9% [Rubino et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224). Lifestyle counseling did not fully replace the drug's appetite effect.

The broader literature points the same way, but the exact pace depends on how much weight was lost, which drug and dose were used, and how long researchers watched. One meta-analysis of eight randomized trials estimated 9.69 kg of regain after semaglutide or tirzepatide, with a wide 95% [confidence interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_interval) of 5.78 to 13.60 kg [Berg et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/obr.13929). Another synthesis of 18 randomized trials found 5.63 kg of average regain among people with overweight or obesity, with extreme between-study variation [Tzang et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2025.103680). A newer nonlinear model estimated that 60% of lost weight returns by one year and that regain may slow below the original baseline, but its long-range curve is partly extrapolated [Budini et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2026.103796).

The studies below show why "you gain it all back" is too blunt.

The 0.8 kg per month figure for newer drugs comes from ten semaglutide or tirzepatide arms; a return to baseline at about 1.5 years was a model projection because follow-up beyond one year was scarce [West et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2025-085304). These are group averages, not a countdown clock.

### Blood sugar, blood pressure, and lipids can drift back too

Semaglutide does more than alter the scale. During treatment it lowers glucose and improves several cardiovascular risk markers, and long trials show fewer major cardiovascular events and kidney outcomes in selected high-risk populations [Kosiborod et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14890), [Lincoff et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1056/nejmoa2307563), [Marso et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1056/nejmoa1607141), [Badve et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/s2213-8587%2824%2900271-7), [Cleto et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41366-024-01646-9). Those outcome trials tested continued treatment. They do not prove that the protection persists after the medication is stopped.

Withdrawal studies mostly measure markers, not heart attacks or kidney failure. Across obesity trials, stopping a GLP-1 drug was followed by an average 0.25 percentage-point rise in [HbA1c](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glycated_hemoglobin), a 0.45 mmol/L rise in fasting glucose, and a 4.15 mm Hg rise in systolic blood pressure; the estimates varied widely between studies [Tzang et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2025.103680). In the STEP 1 extension, blood pressure returned to baseline by one year, while small residual improvements in HbA1c and some lipids remained [Wilding et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725). Among people with type 2 diabetes, pooled HbA1c rose by 0.65 points after GLP-1 therapy stopped [Tzang et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2025.103680).

That distinction matters clinically. Someone taking Ozempic chiefly for diabetes can lose glucose control even if the scale changes little. In one specialized real-world program, HbA1c rose 0.4 points at six months and 0.6 at 12 months after deprescribing, despite no significant average weight regain [McKenzie & Athinarayanan 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13300-024-01547-0). Conversely, STEP 10 showed how strongly active semaglutide can move prediabetes toward normal glucose; once the drug is removed, part of that gain is liable to reverse [McGowan et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/s2213-8587%2824%2900182-7).

Hard outcomes after discontinuation remain uncertain. In an older Veterans Affairs population with chronic kidney disease, gaps of at least 180 days in GLP-1 therapy were associated with higher rates of death, heart attack, heart-failure admission, and stroke [Gregg et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1681/asn.0000000000000477). But that observational association is vulnerable to confounding: people who stop treatment may be sicker, poorer, recently hospitalized, or less able to obtain care. Reviews therefore warn about lost protection without claiming that cessation itself has been proved to trigger an acute cardiovascular rebound [Ceriello et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41574-026-01258-5), [Shah et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.70713).

### The stomach often gets quieter, not "withdrawal sick"

Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, and early fullness are drug effects, especially during dose escalation. Across randomized evidence, gastrointestinal events are much more common with semaglutide than placebo and are the leading reason people stop [Moiz et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.7326/annals-24-01590), [Kommu & Berg 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/obr.13792), [Takrori et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/medicina61111987), [Department of Medicine, St. Luke’s Medical Center, Quezon City, Philippines et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.15605/jafes.037.02.14). In the 17,604-person SELECT trial, gastrointestinal events caused permanent discontinuation in 10.0% on semaglutide versus 2.0% on placebo, mostly during escalation [Kushner et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.24222).

Stopping usually removes that pressure rather than creating a new syndrome. In STEP 4, gastrointestinal events were reported by 49.1% of people who continued semaglutide and 26.1% of those switched to placebo [Rubino et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224). The trials did not identify a characteristic cluster of tremor, sweating, agitation, or other dependence-type symptoms. Appetite returning, however, can feel dramatic precisely because eating had required so little negotiation on treatment.

Persistent severe abdominal pain, repeated vomiting, dehydration, or symptoms that worsen after stopping are not something the withdrawal evidence explains away. They warrant medical assessment rather than being assumed to be normal rebound.

### The turn: regain is common, not destiny

Clinical trials deliberately isolate the drug effect. Real life is messier. In 7,881 patients, one-year weight loss averaged 11.9% among continuers, 6.8% among late discontinuers, and 3.6% among early discontinuers; prediabetes also improved less when treatment ended early [Gasoyan et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.24331). Yet a later cohort of 7,938 discontinuers found only a 0.5% average weight increase in the following year among those treated for obesity - while 19.6% restarted the original drug and 35.2% received another obesity treatment [Gasoyan et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.70660). The small average therefore does not describe untreated cessation.

Selected maintenance studies offer clues, not recipes. Twenty-five women with polycystic ovary syndrome who continued metformin and lifestyle support regained about one-third of their semaglutide loss over two years [Jensterle et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fendo.2024.1366940). In the intensive remote-care cohort noted above, weight remained stable after deprescribing, but patients followed a carbohydrate-restricted program and usually continued metformin [McKenzie & Athinarayanan 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13300-024-01547-0). These uncontrolled or observational results cannot establish that metformin, carbohydrate restriction, or telemedicine will prevent regain for the average person.

Large electronic-record cohorts confirm enormous individual variation and generally more modest weight change than efficacy trials [Alexander et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.70076). The honest answer is probabilistic: regain is the most common trajectory, full regain is not inevitable, and we still cannot reliably predict who will maintain most of the loss.

### Stopping is often an access story - and sometimes temporary

People stop for many reasons besides a planned therapeutic decision. In one cohort, adverse reactions accounted for 26.8% of documented discontinuations and cost for 14.4% [Van Laren et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.70579). Older US claims data found 47.7% had discontinued injectable GLP-1 therapy by one year and 70.1% by two [Weiss et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.2147/ppa.s277676). A UK diabetes cohort found similarly poor persistence, with 45.2% discontinued at one year and 64.7% at two [Weiss et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjdrc-2021-002517).

Restarting is common. Among 125,474 US adults, 53.6% discontinued within one year; among eligible discontinuers, 47.3% with diabetes and 36.3% without it restarted within the next year. Each 1% of weight gained after stopping was associated with a 2.3% to 2.8% higher rate of restarting [Rodriguez et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.57349). This cycle can reflect biology, side effects, shortages, insurance, and income all at once.

### A safe stop is a handoff, not a cliff

No peer-reviewed randomized trial has established that tapering semaglutide prevents weight or glucose rebound. Recent reviews propose gradual reduction and structured transition plans, but they also acknowledge that validated tapering protocols are absent [Ceriello et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41574-026-01258-5), [Shah et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.70713). Because semaglutide already clears slowly, tapering and stopping abruptly are not pharmacologically as different as they would be for a short-acting drug - although a taper may still help some people test appetite, glucose, and weight responses in smaller steps.

The practical question is therefore not simply, "Can I stop?" It is, "What replaces each job the drug was doing?" For weight, that may mean a maintenance plan with food structure, resistance and aerobic activity, sleep, behavioral support, or another therapy. For diabetes, it means arranging glucose monitoring and an alternative medication plan before the last dose, because HbA1c can rise even without visible regain. Weight, waist, blood pressure, and glucose are more useful early-warning instruments than willpower.

The STEP 1 volunteers finished a year off semaglutide lighter than they began, but moving back toward their starting point. That is the clearest image to keep: stopping does not make the body malfunction. It removes a treatment that was actively changing appetite and metabolism. What happens next depends on the old biology, the size of the loss, and what - if anything - takes the drug's place.
