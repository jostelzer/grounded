## Does blue light from screens before bed actually harm sleep?

**Abstract** — Evening screen light acts on the body clock in exactly the way the popular warning says: short-wavelength ("blue") light suppresses the hormone melatonin and pushes circadian timing later, and this has been shown repeatedly under controlled laboratory conditions. What the warning gets wrong is the size of the downstream effect on sleep itself. In experiments, hours of evening screen use delay sleep onset by roughly ten minutes or less, and several well-controlled studies find no change in objectively measured sleep at all. Consistent with this, trials of blue-blocking glasses and screen night modes — interventions that demonstrably remove the offending wavelengths — mostly fail to improve measured sleep in healthy adults; a Cochrane review rates the evidence as very low certainty, and meta-analytic estimates straddle zero. The averages, however, conceal real variation: sensitivity of the circadian system to evening light differs several-fold between individuals, and people with insomnia, delayed sleep phase, or mood disorders appear to benefit more from blocking evening light than healthy sleepers do. The fair summary is that the mechanism is real, the typical harm is small, and the time displaced by the screen — a later bedtime — matters more for sleep than the colour of its light.

### Introduction

Few sleep-hygiene claims are repeated as confidently as "blue light before bed ruins your sleep", and it is built on genuine physiology: the eye's circadian photoreception is tuned to short wavelengths, and evening light of that kind suppresses [melatonin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melatonin) and delays the internal clock. The contested step is the inference from hormone to harm — whether the melatonin effect translates into meaningfully worse sleep, and whether filtering blue light therefore improves it. This review takes the question in three steps: the mechanism, the measured sleep outcomes, and the filtering interventions, before asking for whom the answer differs.

### The mechanism

Evening short-wavelength light reliably suppresses melatonin and shifts circadian timing. In an inpatient crossover study (n=12, 5 evenings per condition), four hours of reading on a light-emitting e-reader suppressed melatonin by **55%** and delayed [dim-light melatonin onset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dim-light_melatonin_onset) by more than 1.5 h relative to a print book (p<0.001) [Chang et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1418490112). A five-hour session in front of an LED-backlit screen emitting twice as much 464 nm light as a non-LED comparator likewise suppressed the evening melatonin rise and reduced sleepiness (crossover, n=13) [Cajochen et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00165.2011). The pathway runs through the [melanopsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melanopsin) system rather than ordinary vision: at home-realistic intensities, the melatonin profile and EEG-defined sleep onset tracked the melanopic rather than the photopic content of the light (six-way crossover, n=22) [Santhi et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-079x.2011.00970.x).

### Measured sleep

The sleep that follows this hormonal disruption is only modestly different. In the e-reader study, the cost was about **10 minutes** of extra sleep latency (25.7 vs 15.8 min, p=0.009) and roughly 12 minutes less REM — a delayed night rather than a wrecked one [Chang et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1418490112). Shorter, more realistic exposures do even less: thirty minutes of iPad reading in bed delayed [slow-wave activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slow-wave_sleep) by about 30 minutes but changed neither sleep onset latency nor sleep timing on [polysomnography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polysomnography) (crossover, n=16) [Grønli et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2016.02.006), and two hours of evening tablet reading after a bright day altered no melatonin or polysomnographic measure at all compared with a paper book (crossover, n=14) [Rångtell et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2016.06.016). Even removing the blue component from ambient bedroom light (50 [lux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lux) for 8 h) reduced melatonin suppression without changing total sleep time, efficiency, or slow-wave activity (inpatient crossover, n=16) [Rahman et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.physbeh.2017.05.002).

Observational data at scale point the same way. Across 21 cohorts totalling 548,338 people, each extra hour of daily screen time was associated with only **3–5 minutes** less sleep (β = −0.05, 95% [CI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_interval) −0.08 to −0.03) [He et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1640263), and within individuals daily screen use predicted a later sleep onset (r = 0.079, 95% CI 0.010–0.149) but not duration, latency, efficiency, or quality (25 studies, 4,562 youth) [Bourke et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2025.6490).

### Filtering interventions

If blue light were the active ingredient, removing it should help — and optically, removal works: night-mode settings cut estimated melatonin suppression by up to **93%** and blue-reducing lenses by about 33% [Teran et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1097/opx.0000000000001485). The sleep benefit does not reliably follow. The table below gathers the trial evidence on filtering.

A consistent pattern runs through these trials: self-reported sleep improves slightly while instrumented sleep does not [Bigalke et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleh.2021.02.004), [Shechter et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleepadvances/zpaa002).

### Who is affected

Against the null average stands genuine individual variation. Sensitivity of the circadian system to evening light differs widely between people and is stable within them (intra-class correlation 0.44), so group means hide a subset whose clocks do shift substantially [Santhi et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-079x.2011.00970.x), [Chellappa 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsaa214). The clearest benefits of blocking evening light appear in disordered sleep — insomnia, delayed sleep phase, shift work, and bipolar disorder — rather than in healthy sleepers [Hester et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/07420528.2021.1930029). Context modulates the effect too: after a day of bright light exposure, the evening tablet effect disappeared entirely [Rångtell et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2016.06.016). In children the story is similar in miniature — partial blue-blockers advanced sleep onset by about 6 minutes in 10–12-year-olds with no change in salivary melatonin (crossover, n=39) [Maeda-Nishino et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0332877), and interventions cutting children's screen time by 33 min/day added only 11 minutes of sleep (95% CI 0.05–0.33 h; 11 trials, 4,656 children) [Martin et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13130).

### Conclusion

The physiology behind the warning is solid; the warning itself is inflated. Evening screen light measurably suppresses melatonin and delays the clock, yet the resulting sleep cost in experiments is around ten minutes, and removing the blue component — by glasses, night modes, or spectrally tuned lighting — leaves objectively measured sleep essentially unchanged in healthy adults. Where evening light does matter is at the margins: in light-sensitive individuals and in clinical sleep and mood disorders, where blocking it shows more promise. For most people, the actionable variable is not the spectrum of the screen but the half hour of sleep it displaces. What would settle the open questions is a well-powered trial that stratifies participants by measured light sensitivity and tracks objective sleep at home — until then, "blue light ruins sleep" remains a mechanism in search of a matching effect size.
