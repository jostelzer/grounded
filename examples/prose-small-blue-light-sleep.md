## Does blue light from screens before bed actually harm sleep?

**Abstract** — Evening short-wavelength ("blue") light suppresses melatonin and delays circadian timing, an effect repeatedly demonstrated under controlled laboratory conditions. The downstream effect on sleep is smaller than commonly claimed. In experimental studies, several hours of evening screen use delay sleep onset by roughly ten minutes or less, and several well-controlled studies find no change in objectively measured sleep. Trials of blue-blocking glasses and screen night modes — interventions that substantially reduce short-wavelength exposure — mostly do not improve measured sleep in healthy adults; a Cochrane review rates the evidence as very low certainty, and meta-analytic estimates include zero. These averages conceal real variation: sensitivity of the circadian system to evening light differs several-fold between individuals, and people with insomnia, delayed sleep phase, or mood disorders appear to benefit more from blocking evening light than healthy sleepers do. In summary, the mechanism is established, the typical effect on sleep in healthy adults is small, and the later bedtime associated with screen use accounts for more sleep loss than the spectral composition of the light.

### Introduction

The circadian system is maximally sensitive to short-wavelength light, and evening exposure of this kind suppresses [melatonin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melatonin) and delays the internal clock. Whether this hormonal effect translates into meaningfully worse sleep — and whether filtering blue light therefore improves sleep — remains contested. This review examines the mechanism, the measured sleep outcomes, and the filtering interventions, then considers for whom the effects differ.

### The mechanism

Evening short-wavelength light reliably suppresses melatonin and shifts circadian timing. In an inpatient crossover study (n=12, 5 evenings per condition), four hours of reading on a light-emitting e-reader suppressed melatonin by **55%** and delayed [dim-light melatonin onset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dim-light_melatonin_onset) by more than 1.5 h relative to a print book (p<0.001) [Chang et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1418490112). A five-hour session in front of an LED-backlit screen emitting twice as much 464 nm light as a non-LED comparator likewise suppressed the evening melatonin rise and reduced sleepiness (crossover, n=13) [Cajochen et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00165.2011). The pathway runs through the [melanopsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melanopsin) system rather than ordinary vision: at home-realistic intensities, the melatonin profile and EEG-defined sleep onset tracked the melanopic rather than the photopic content of the light (six-way crossover, n=22) [Santhi et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-079x.2011.00970.x).

### Measured sleep

The corresponding effects on sleep are modest. In the e-reader study, sleep latency increased by about **10 minutes** (25.7 vs 15.8 min, p=0.009) and REM sleep decreased by roughly 12 minutes [Chang et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1418490112). Shorter exposures produce smaller effects: thirty minutes of iPad reading in bed delayed [slow-wave activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slow-wave_sleep) by about 30 minutes but changed neither sleep onset latency nor sleep timing on [polysomnography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polysomnography) (crossover, n=16) [Grønli et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2016.02.006), and two hours of evening tablet reading after a bright day altered no melatonin or polysomnographic measure compared with a paper book (crossover, n=14) [Rångtell et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2016.06.016). Removing the blue component from ambient bedroom light (50 [lux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lux) for 8 h) reduced melatonin suppression without changing total sleep time, efficiency, or slow-wave activity (inpatient crossover, n=16) [Rahman et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.physbeh.2017.05.002).

Observational data show similarly small associations. Across 21 cohorts totalling 548,338 people, each extra hour of daily screen time was associated with **3–5 minutes** less sleep (β = −0.05, 95% [CI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_interval) −0.08 to −0.03) [He et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1640263), and within individuals daily screen use predicted a later sleep onset (r = 0.079, 95% CI 0.010–0.149) but not duration, latency, efficiency, or quality (25 studies, 4,562 youth) [Bourke et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2025.6490).

### Filtering interventions

Filtering removes the short-wavelength exposure effectively: night-mode settings reduce estimated melatonin suppression by up to **93%** and blue-reducing lenses by about 33% [Teran et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1097/opx.0000000000001485). A corresponding improvement in sleep is not reliably observed. The table below summarises the trial evidence on filtering.

A consistent pattern runs through these trials: self-reported sleep improves slightly while instrumented sleep does not [Bigalke et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleh.2021.02.004), [Shechter et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleepadvances/zpaa002).

### Who is affected

Individual variation qualifies the null average. Sensitivity of the circadian system to evening light differs widely between people and is stable within them (intra-class correlation 0.44), so group means conceal a subset with substantial circadian shifts [Santhi et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-079x.2011.00970.x), [Chellappa 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsaa214). The clearest benefits of blocking evening light appear in disordered sleep — insomnia, delayed sleep phase, shift work, and bipolar disorder — rather than in healthy sleepers [Hester et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/07420528.2021.1930029). Prior light history also modulates the effect: after a day of bright light exposure, the evening tablet effect disappeared [Rångtell et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2016.06.016). In children, partial blue-blockers advanced sleep onset by about 6 minutes in 10–12-year-olds with no change in salivary melatonin (crossover, n=39) [Maeda-Nishino et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0332877), and interventions that reduced children's screen time by 33 min/day added 11 minutes of sleep (95% CI 0.05–0.33 h; 11 trials, 4,656 children) [Martin et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13130).

### Conclusion

Evening screen light suppresses melatonin and delays circadian timing, but the resulting sleep cost in experiments is around ten minutes, and removing the blue component — by glasses, night modes, or spectrally tuned lighting — leaves objectively measured sleep essentially unchanged in healthy adults. Benefits of blocking evening light are more plausible in light-sensitive individuals and in clinical sleep and mood disorders. For most people, the sleep displaced by a later bedtime exceeds any effect of the screen's spectrum. A well-powered trial that stratifies participants by measured light sensitivity and tracks objective sleep at home would resolve the remaining uncertainty.
