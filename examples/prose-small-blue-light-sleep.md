## Does blue light from screens before bed meaningfully harm sleep?

**Abstract** — Evening screen light can suppress the hormone that signals biological night and delay the internal clock, but the resulting loss of sleep is usually smaller and less consistent than popular claims imply. A demanding inpatient experiment found about ten minutes longer to fall asleep after four hours of maximum-brightness e-reader use, whereas shorter exposures under other conditions often changed neither sleep duration nor quality. This distinction matters because a hormonal response is not itself evidence of impaired sleep. Trials of blue-light-blocking glasses reinforce the uncertainty: objective measurements generally show little or no benefit, although some people with insomnia report improvement. The best-supported conclusion is therefore conditional. Long, bright exposure close to bedtime can delay sleep, while effects from ordinary use depend on duration, brightness, earlier daytime light, and individual sensitivity. Blue-light filters are not established sleep treatments for healthy people.

### Introduction

The claim that screens “ruin” sleep combines several different questions: whether evening light affects biology, whether that response changes the following sleep episode, and whether removing short wavelengths improves sleep. Evening short-wavelength light can alter [melatonin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melatonin), alertness, and [circadian rhythm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circadian_rhythm), but these outcomes cannot be treated as interchangeable with sleep duration or quality. [Cajochen et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00165.2011), [Santhi et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-079x.2011.00970.x) The strongest evidence comes from controlled crossover experiments and randomized filter trials, which reveal a real mechanism but inconsistent downstream effects.

### Circadian and sleep effects

Screen spectrum affects evening physiology. In a balanced crossover study of 13 young men, five hours before an LED screen with more than twice the 464-nm emission of a non-LED comparison suppressed the melatonin rise and reduced both subjective and objective sleepiness. The study demonstrated a spectrum-sensitive alerting response, but it did not measure the subsequent sleep episode. [Cajochen et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00165.2011) In a six-condition crossover experiment (n=22), melatonin, sleepiness, and sleep onset varied with home-relevant spectra and largely tracked the [melanopsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melanopsin) system; sensitivity also differed reproducibly between individuals. [Santhi et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-079x.2011.00970.x)

The clearest adverse sleep result followed an unusually long exposure. Twelve healthy adults read for four hours at maximum e-reader brightness on five consecutive evenings during an inpatient crossover study. Compared with print, e-reader use suppressed melatonin by **55.12%**, delayed its evening timing by more than 1.5 hours, and increased objectively measured sleep latency from 15.75 to 25.65 minutes. [Rapid-eye-movement sleep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapid_eye_movement_sleep) fell by 11.82 minutes, but total sleep time, sleep efficiency, and non-rapid-eye-movement sleep did not change. Because the illuminated and printed conditions differed in both irradiance and spectrum, the study could not attribute the effect to blue wavelengths alone. [Chang et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1418490112)

More selective and shorter experiments weaken a simple causal story. A preregistered crossover study exposed 29 healthy adults to two visually matched light conditions differing twofold in melanopsin-weighted input for one hour, ending 50 minutes before bed. The higher condition suppressed melatonin by about **14%**, yet changed neither sleepiness nor vigilance, subjective sleep, sleep architecture, or any [polysomnography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polysomnography) outcome. [Blume et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsac199) Likewise, after 6.5 hours of bright daytime light, two hours of evening tablet versus print reading altered neither melatonin nor objectively recorded sleep in 14 adults. [Rångtell et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2016.06.016) These findings indicate that exposure duration, prior light, and the contrast between conditions materially affect results.

### Filtering interventions

If short-wavelength light were the principal cause of meaningful sleep loss, filtering it should reliably improve sleep. Trial evidence does not show that pattern. A Cochrane review identified six sleep trials involving 148 participants: three reported better sleep scores and three did not. Differences in populations and outcomes, incomplete masking, and other bias risks led to a **very-low-certainty** [GRADE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GRADE_approach) judgment. [Singh et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.cd013244.pub2)

Objective synthesis is similarly inconclusive. A 2025 meta-analysis of three double-blind crossover trials (n=49) found sleep onset 4.86 minutes earlier with blue-blocking glasses, but the 95% [confidence interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_interval) ranged from 20.23 minutes earlier to 10.52 minutes later. Total sleep increased by 8.75 minutes, with an interval from 35.31 minutes less to 52.82 minutes more; sleep efficiency and wake after sleep onset were also unchanged on [actigraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actigraphy). [Luna-Rangel et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fneur.2025.1699303) An earlier review of 12 small studies likewise found modest, uncertain objective effects but larger self-reported improvements, suggesting that perception and instrumented sleep do not consistently agree. [Shechter et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleepadvances/zpaa002)

Individual trials illustrate that split. In 20 healthy adults, one week of blue-blocking versus clear lenses reduced reported sleep onset by three minutes, yet objective sleep did not improve and total sleep tended to be shorter with the filter. [Bigalke et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleh.2021.02.004) In 60 healthy pregnant participants, full versus partial blue blocking changed actigraphic total sleep by 13.0 minutes (95% interval −9.5 to 35.5) and efficiency by 1.7 percentage points (−0.4 to 3.7), neither a clear benefit. [Liset et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0262799) By contrast, a crossover pilot in 14 adults with insomnia symptoms improved insomnia ratings and actigraphic total sleep after one week of amber lenses. [Shechter et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychires.2017.10.015) That clinical signal is plausible but too small to establish who benefits.

### Conclusion

Evening screen light can delay biological timing and sleep onset, especially when exposure is long and bright. It does not follow that typical screen use causes large sleep loss, or that melatonin suppression predicts worse sleep. The objective filter literature is small, heterogeneous, and mostly compatible with effects ranging from modest benefit to none. For healthy sleepers, blue-light-blocking glasses are not a reliable evidence-based sleep treatment; possible benefits in insomnia require larger preregistered trials that measure light at the eye and objective sleep.
