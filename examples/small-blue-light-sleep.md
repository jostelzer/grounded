## Does blue light from screens before bed actually harm sleep?

**TL;DR** — Partly. Evening light really does suppress the sleep hormone and push the body clock later. But the leap to "ruins your sleep" is not supported: experiments find sleep effects of about ten minutes or less, and blue-blocking glasses and night modes do not reliably improve measured sleep. Bedtime itself matters more than the colour of the light.

### The mechanism is real: evening short-wavelength light suppresses melatonin and delays the clock

- Four hours of evening reading on a light-emitting e-reader suppressed [melatonin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melatonin) by **55%** and pushed [dim-light melatonin onset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dim-light_melatonin_onset) >1.5 h later than print (inpatient crossover, n=12, 5 evenings per condition, p<0.001) [Chang et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1418490112).
- A 5-h LED-backlit screen with twice the 464 nm emission of a non-LED screen suppressed the evening melatonin rise and cut sleepiness (crossover, n=13) [Cajochen et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00165.2011).
- The pathway is the [melanopsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melanopsin) system, not vision: at home-realistic intensities, melatonin rise and EEG sleep onset tracked melanopic rather than photopic content (six-way crossover, n=22) [Santhi et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-079x.2011.00970.x).

### The sleep that follows is barely different

- In that study the cost was **~10 min** longer to fall asleep (25.7 vs 15.8 min, p=0.009) and ~12 min less REM — a delayed night, not a wrecked one [Chang et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1418490112).
- Thirty minutes of iPad reading in bed delayed [slow-wave activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slow-wave_sleep) ~30 min but changed **neither** sleep onset latency nor sleep timing on [polysomnography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polysomnography) (crossover, n=16) [Grønli et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2016.02.006).
- Two hours of evening tablet reading after a bright day altered no melatonin or polysomnographic measure versus a paper book (crossover, n=14) [Rångtell et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2016.06.016).
- Blue-depleted bedroom light (50 [lux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lux), 8 h) reduced melatonin suppression yet left total sleep time, efficiency and slow-wave activity unchanged (inpatient crossover, n=16) [Rahman et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.physbeh.2017.05.002).
- Each extra hour of daily screen time tracks **3–5 min** less sleep (β = −0.05, 95% [CI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_interval) −0.08 to −0.03; 21 cohorts, 548,338 people) [He et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1640263); within individuals it predicts later sleep onset (r = 0.079, 95% CI 0.010–0.149) and nothing else — not duration, latency, efficiency or quality (25 studies, 4,562 youth) [Bourke et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2025.6490).

### Filtering the light works optically; the sleep benefit does not follow

- Night modes cut estimated melatonin suppression by up to **93%**, blue-reducing lenses by ~33%; the optics are not in doubt [Teran et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1097/opx.0000000000001485).

### It matters more for some sleepers than for most

- Circadian light sensitivity varies widely between people and is stable within them (intra-class correlation 0.44), so averages hide those whose clock does shift [Santhi et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-079x.2011.00970.x), [Chellappa 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsaa214).
- Benefits of blocking evening light are clearest in disordered sleep — insomnia, delayed sleep phase, shift work, bipolar disorder — not in healthy sleepers [Hester et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/07420528.2021.1930029).
- Daytime light history matters: after a bright day the tablet effect vanished [Rångtell et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2016.06.016).
- In 10–12-year-olds, partial blue-blockers advanced sleep onset ~6 min with **no change in salivary melatonin** (crossover, n=39) [Maeda-Nishino et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0332877).
- Cutting children's screen time by 33 min/day added just 11 min of sleep (95% CI 0.05–0.33 h; 11 trials, 4,656 children) [Martin et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13130).
