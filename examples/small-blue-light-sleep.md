## Does blue light before bed ruin sleep?

**TL;DR** — No—not in the sweeping sense. Evening light rich in short wavelengths can delay the body clock and sometimes sleep, especially when exposure is bright, long, and close to bedtime; ordinary screen exposures produce smaller, inconsistent objective sleep changes, and filters have not reliably improved sleep.

### The biological signal is real, but it is not the same as lost sleep

- Narrow-band 469-nm light suppressed [melatonin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melatonin) in a dose-dependent curve (night-time experiment, n=8), while a synthesis of 19 laboratory datasets found that light weighted for [melanopsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melanopsin) sensitivity best predicted circadian responses across spectra, intensities, and durations. [West et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.01413.2009), [Brown 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/jpi.12655)
- Five hours at a blue-enriched computer display suppressed melatonin and sleepiness relative to another white display (crossover, n=13), but the study did not measure the following sleep episode. [Cajochen et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00165.2011)
- A cleaner test isolated melanopic input: doubling it for one hour suppressed melatonin by about **14%**, yet changed neither sleepiness nor any [polysomnography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polysomnography) measure (preregistered crossover, n=29). [Blume et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsac199)

### Screen experiments show a dose-and-timing pattern, not universal damage

| Exposure | Design | Objective sleep result |
|---|---|---|
| E-reader vs print, 4 h before bed for 5 evenings | Inpatient crossover, n=12 | Sleep onset was **9.9 min later** and rapid-eye-movement sleep **11.8 min shorter**; total sleep and efficiency were unchanged. [Chang et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1418490112) |
| Tablet vs print, 2 h after 6.5 h of bright daytime light | Crossover, n=14 | No difference in melatonin or recorded sleep. [Rångtell et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2016.06.016) |
| Visually matched displays, 3.5 h across screen-like intensities | Crossover within four intensity groups, n=72 men | Lower melanopic light shortened sleep latency only at the brightest setting; latency and melatonin followed dose-response relationships. [Schöllhorn et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-023-04598-4) |
| Smartphone, smartphone night filter, or print for 90 min; stopped ~50 min before bed | Three-night crossover, n=68 males | Whole-night sleep and memory were unchanged; unfiltered light slightly reduced early deep sleep in adults, not adolescents. [Höhn et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/braincomms/fcae173) |

### What people do on screens may matter as much as the colour of the light

- In 79 youths observed at home, screen use during the two hours before bed was not associated with total sleep, but each extra 10 minutes of interactive use in bed was associated with **9 minutes less sleep** (95% [confidence interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_interval) 2–16 minutes less). This study could not isolate light from delayed bedtime, content, or interaction. [Brosnan et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2024.2914)
- Evening-light sensitivity varies markedly: among 55 adults, the light level estimated to halve melatonin ranged from **6 to 350 lux**; home monitoring likewise found responses from 0% to 87% suppression under the average home’s light. [Phillips et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1901824116), [Cain et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-75622-4)
- In a six-condition crossover (n=22), home-relevant spectra changed melatonin and sleep onset, but individual sensitivity was only moderately repeatable. No single colour or brightness threshold fits everyone. [Santhi et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-079x.2011.00970.x)

### Filters change the stimulus, but dependable sleep benefits have not followed

- A Cochrane review found mixed sleep results across six randomized trials (n=148) and rated certainty **very low**; a later [actigraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actigraphy) meta-analysis of three crossover trials (n=49) estimated sleep onset **4.86 min earlier** (95% interval −20.23 to +10.52) and total sleep **8.75 min longer** (−35.31 to +52.82)—both compatible with benefit or harm. [Singh et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.cd013244.pub2), [Luna-Rangel et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fneur.2025.1699303)
- In healthy adults (crossover, n=20), blue-blocking glasses improved a few self-reports but no objective sleep outcome; objective total sleep was 433 vs 449 minutes with clear lenses. [Bigalke et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleh.2021.02.004)
- A positive insomnia pilot increased actigraphic total sleep (crossover, n=14), but it is too small to establish treatment efficacy. Taken together, filters may help selected poor sleepers, but “blue light ruins sleep” overstates both the average effect and the certainty. [Shechter et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychires.2017.10.015), [Luna-Rangel et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fneur.2025.1699303)
