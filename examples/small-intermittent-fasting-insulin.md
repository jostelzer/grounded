## Does intermittent fasting improve insulin sensitivity?

**TL;DR** — Sometimes, but probably only a little. Benefits are clearest against unrestricted eating, not equal calorie reduction. Early eating may add a short-term effect, but direct measurements and longer follow-up do not show a durable, weight-independent advantage.

### The average benefit is small, and the comparator changes the answer

- In adults with overweight or obesity, intermittent fasting lowered fasting insulin versus no diet intervention by a small [standardised mean difference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standardized_mean_difference) of −0.21 (95% [confidence interval (CI)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_interval) −0.40 to −0.02); few favourable findings were high-certainty [Sun et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102519).
- Across 99 randomised trials (n=6,582), fasting and continuous calorie restriction had broadly similar cardiometabolic effects, with no difference in [glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glycated_hemoglobin); alternate-day fasting produced only a small extra weight reduction versus continuous restriction (−1.29 kg, 95% CI −1.99 to −0.59) [Semnani-Azad et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2024-082007).
- In prediabetes or type 2 diabetes (10 trials, n=599), time-restricted eating lowered fasting glucose by 0.30 mmol/L (95% CI 0.07 to 0.53 lower) and weight by 1.6 kg, but not overall HbA1c; effects on insulin sensitivity were inconclusive [Viple et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00125-026-06792-5).

### Matching calories largely removes a fasting-specific advantage

- A meta-analysis of 20 same-calorie trials (n=1,785) found no overall advantage for fasting glucose, HbA1c, or [homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeostatic_model_assessment); long-term subgroups were sparse and no included trial was at low risk of bias [Hamsho et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.numecd.2024.103805).
- Adding an early 8-hour window to the same prescribed calorie restriction did not improve metabolic risk factors at 12 months (n=139); under fully provided isocaloric diets, time restriction also did not improve fasting glucose, insulin resistance, or glucose tolerance at 12 weeks (n=41) [Liu et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1056/nejmoa2114833), [Maruthur et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.7326/m23-3132).
- In 88 women, matched energy restriction produced no between-group difference in insulin sensitivity measured by a [hyperinsulinaemic–euglycaemic clamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucose_clamp_technique); a 24-hour fast transiently reduced sensitivity, showing that test timing can reverse the apparent effect [Hutchison et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.22345).

### Early eating is promising, but positive results have not replicated consistently

- A weight-stable controlled-feeding crossover trial found improved oral-glucose-test-derived insulin sensitivity after five weeks of a 6-hour early window, but only eight men completed it and fasting before testing differed between conditions [Sutton et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2018.04.010).
- An intended-isocaloric crossover trial in 31 women found no insulin-sensitivity improvement after either early or late 8-hour eating; in 14 adults with type 2 diabetes, 10-hour eating lowered 24-hour glucose (6.8 versus 7.6 mmol/L) without improving hepatic or peripheral clamp sensitivity [Peters et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1126/scitranslmed.adv6787), [Andriessen et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00125-022-05752-z).
- Early intermittent fasting improved mixed-meal glucose exposure more than continuous restriction at six months in 209 adults at diabetes risk, but the difference disappeared by 18 months; glucose tolerance is not a direct measure of insulin sensitivity [Teong et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-023-02287-7).

### Diabetes outcomes may improve, but usually with energy reduction, weight loss, or clinical support

| Population and regimen | Duration | Main result | Evidence |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Type 2 diabetes; 5:2 versus daily restriction (n=137) | 12 months | HbA1c reductions were equivalent (−0.3% versus −0.5%) | [Carter et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2018.0756) |
| Type 2 diabetes; 8-hour eating versus restriction or control (n=75) | 6 months | HbA1c fell similarly with fasting (−0.91%) and restriction (−0.94%); fasting also cut reported intake by 313 kcal/day | [Pavlou et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2023.39337) |
| Insulin-treated type 2 diabetes; three fasting days/week (n=46) | 12 weeks | HbA1c fell by 7.3 mmol/mol versus 0.1 with control; no severe hypoglycaemia under monitoring and medication adjustment | [Obermayer et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc22-1622) |

- Across 15 trials (n=1,365), fasting did not increase fatigue, headache, or dropout, but evidence for common harms was low-certainty and long-term, regimen-specific safety remains uncertain [Zhong et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12937-024-00975-9).
- Adherence is not automatically easier: one-year dropout was 38% with alternate-day fasting versus 29% with daily restriction, and fasting did not improve fasting insulin or insulin resistance [Trepanowski et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2017.0936).
