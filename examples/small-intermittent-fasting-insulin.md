## Does intermittent fasting improve insulin sensitivity?

**TL;DR** — Modestly, and mostly through weight loss. Compared with not dieting, intermittent fasting produces small improvements in fasting insulin and insulin resistance; compared with ordinary daily calorie restriction it is no better. An extra, weight-independent benefit from eating earlier in the day rests on very small trials with conflicting results.

### Against no diet, fasting lowers insulin measures — modestly

- In adults with overweight or obesity, intermittent fasting reduced fasting insulin by an [SMD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standardized_mean_difference) of −0.21 (95% [CI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_interval) −0.40 to −0.02; 13 trials, high [GRADE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GRADE_approach) certainty) and [HOMA-IR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeostatic_model_assessment) by −0.35 (−0.65 to −0.04) versus a non-intervention diet [Sun et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102519).
- A [network meta-analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_meta-analysis) of 99 trials (n=6,582) found trivial reductions in fasting glucose and HOMA-IR for **every** diet strategy — fasting variants and continuous restriction alike — versus ad-libitum eating, and no improvement in [HbA1c](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glycated_hemoglobin) anywhere [Semnani-Azad et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2024-082007).
- In metabolic syndrome, pooled trials show fasting glucose −0.15 mmol/L (−0.23 to −0.06) and HOMA-IR −0.31 (−0.44 to −0.19) after intermittent fasting [Yuan et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1155/2022/6999907).
- An umbrella review rates most such glycaemic associations low or very-low quality; the high-certainty benefits are anthropometric [Patikorn et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2021.39558).

### Against matched calorie restriction, the advantage largely disappears

- Alternate-day fasting for 12 months (RCT, n=100) changed fasting glucose, insulin, and HOMA-IR no more than daily calorie restriction [Trepanowski et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2017.0936).
- A 28-trial meta-analysis found no differences from continuous restriction in glucose, insulin, or HOMA-IR, though its alternate-day-fasting subset improved HOMA-IR slightly more [Schroor et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.advnut.2023.10.003); high-certainty umbrella-review comparisons against continuous restriction are likewise null [Sun et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102519).
- The main exception: in 107 overweight women, a 5:2 diet cut fasting insulin −1.2 µU/mL (−1.4 to −1.0) more than continuous restriction at equal weight loss (6-mo RCT) [Harvie et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1038/ijo.2010.171).

### An early eating window may act independently of weight — but the trials are tiny and split

- In the only eucaloric crossover trial of early time-restricted feeding (8 completers, men with [prediabetes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prediabetes), 5 wk, weight held stable), a 6-h window ending mid-afternoon lowered fasting insulin 3.4 ± 1.6 mU/L and mean 3-h [OGTT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucose_tolerance_test) insulin 26 ± 9 mU/L, with no change in fasting glucose [Sutton et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2018.04.010).
- Direct measurement disagrees: 3 weeks of 10-h eating in type 2 diabetes (crossover, n=14) lowered 24-h glucose (6.8 ± 0.2 vs 7.6 ± 0.3 mmol/L) and added ~3 h/day in the normal glucose range, yet [euglycaemic-clamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucose_clamp_technique) insulin sensitivity did not change [Andriessen et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00125-022-05752-z).
- Two null trials weigh against a free-standing timing effect: isocaloric 10-h eating for 12 weeks (RCT, n=41) changed neither weight nor any glycaemic measure [Maruthur et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.7326/m23-3132), and 16:8 eating without other intervention (RCT, n=116) left fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, and HbA1c unchanged [Lowe et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2020.4153).

### In type 2 diabetes it matches conventional dieting and appears safe

- A 5:2 diet was equivalent to daily restriction for HbA1c over 12 months (RCT, n=137, prespecified ±0.5% margin) [Carter et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2018.0756); pooled trials give a between-diet HbA1c difference of −0.06% (−0.27 to 0.16) [Wang et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.diabres.2021.109003).
- 8-h time-restricted eating without calorie counting lowered HbA1c by −0.91% (−1.61 to −0.20) versus control over 6 months — no different from daily calorie counting (RCT, n=75) [Pavlou et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2023.39337).
- Even in insulin-treated diabetes, fasting 3 days/week cut HbA1c by 7.3 ± 12.0 mmol/mol versus +0.1 ± 6.1 in controls, with no severe hypoglycaemia (12-wk RCT, n=46) [Obermayer et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc22-1622).
- What would settle it: adequately powered trials of early versus late eating windows at matched energy intake, with clamp-based insulin-sensitivity endpoints rather than fasting surrogates.
