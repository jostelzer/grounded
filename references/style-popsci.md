# Popsci style (explicit)

Use when the user asks for `popsci`, "popular science", "magazine style", "science journalism", or names a magazine of that register (Scientific American, New Scientist, Quanta, Nautilus). The pipeline, evidence standard, source counts, citations, and verification are identical to every other style — popsci changes the register and the architecture of the telling, nothing underneath. The model is the feature well of a great popular-science magazine: a piece a curious adult reads for pleasure and finishes knowing exactly what science does and does not say — with the one thing no magazine gives them, a checkable citation on every claim. The shared structure, language, citing, and quality-gate rules in `writing-guide.md` apply in full; popsci replaces only the register rules, as defined here.

**From the synthesis** (`synthesis.md`, per `synthesis-guide.md`): the beats along the spine are synthesis claims in narrative order; each section's star number is drawn from its claim's `numbers` field and the rest stays behind; the turn is the claim or pattern whose contrary line bites hardest; the kicker's forward look comes from the synthesis's Open entries. The claims, strengths, and contrary evidence are fixed before storytelling begins — the spine decides only the telling.

Structure:

```
## <Headline — the question recast as a magazine title: concrete, curious, honest>

*<Standfirst — 1–3 citation-free sentences under the headline, italic: state the
question plainly and preview the stakes and the shape of the answer. It sets up
the piece; it does not compress it — the beats, the numbers, and above all the
turn stay in the body. If the headline names a thing by one name, the standfirst
keeps that name; a synonym (brand → generic, common name → species) may appear
only after the body has established they are the same. This replaces the
abstract.>*

<The lede: a cold open of 1–2 short paragraphs — a concrete moment from a real
study, a striking verified fact, or the place the question shows up in the
reader's life. Cited like all body text.>

<The nut graf: one paragraph that widens the shot — why the question matters,
who it touches and at what scale, what is claimed and contested, and what the
piece sets out to find. This is where the throughline is posed.>

### <Crosshead — a narrative beat, evocative but honest>
<Sections as story beats along the spine. See rules below.>

### <The turn's own crosshead — name where the evidence complicates, never the
word "turn" itself>
<The contrary or null evidence, told as the twist it is.>

### <The kicker>
<Circle back to the opening image, say plainly where the evidence lands and at
what confidence, and end on the specific thing that would settle what is open —
a forward-looking last line, not a shrug and not a recap.>

**Sources**
<same generated sources block as always>
```

The story — what makes it a feature rather than a review in costume:

- **Choose the spine before drafting.** A feature is organized by narrative logic, never by outcome category. Pick one spine and commit: **chronology** (what happens first, then, later — a question about change over time is told in time), **the investigation** (how researchers worked it out, finding by finding, disagreement by disagreement), or **a followed subject** (one trial, cohort, or place tracked from start to finish, with the wider literature arriving as context). Every crosshead is a beat along that spine. The test: if the sections could be reordered without breaking anything, the piece is a topical review wearing crossheads — reorganize before writing, not after.
- **A numbers budget, at magazine density.** Each section *leads* with at most one or two numbers, translated to human scale; every other statistic moves into a parenthesis, a table, or gets rounded in prose ("about half", "two-thirds", "one in five"). Three sentences in a row each carrying statistics is scientific style leaking through. And the budget binds the whole piece, not just the leads: a real magazine feature runs on the order of *single-digit numbers per hundred sentences* — most findings arrive as verbal quantities ("ate about a quarter less at lunch", "roughly a tenth of their body weight") with the digits living in the table, the synthesis, and the sources. Nothing is lost by the budget — the prose carries only the numbers the reader must feel.
- **The narrator is a reporter** — never a counselor, never a poet. Every claim about how something feels, what people experience, or what should be done carries a citation or an explicit attribution ("participants reported…", "clinicians describe…", "reviews recommend…"); practical guidance is reported from the sources, never prescribed to the reader in imperative or advice-column voice. Imagery follows the one-metaphor rule below and never carries an empirical claim on its own. When a passage drifts lyrical or advisory, the fix is a source or a cut, not better prose.

The storytelling — how the magazines actually do it:

- **The headline is honest curiosity.** Recast the question so a browsing reader wants the answer ("Does creatine treat depression?" → "The gym supplement that might lift mood"). It may intrigue; it may not overclaim, tease a payoff the evidence doesn't deliver, or promise certainty the kicker walks back. A null result can still headline — make the absence the story ("The memory pill that never was").
- **Open close, then widen.** The lede is a close-up: one trial's setup, one measured moment, one number that surprises. The nut graf pulls back to the whole literature and poses the throughline. That zoom — close-up on a study, wide shot across the field, back in close — is the piece's basic camera work, and it is what separates a feature from a summary.
- **Studies are events; people do things.** "In 2022, a team at Oxford followed 8,376 teenagers for a year" — findings arrive as moments in an unfolding investigation, with researchers, cohorts, and instruments as the actors. This applies to meta-analyses too: someone pooled the trials, and the pooling is an event with a motive. Use only details that are actually in the paper: sample sizes, settings, methods, years, locations. **Never invent colour** — no imagined patients, composite characters, weather, or lab scenes the methods section doesn't contain. A hypothetical is allowed only when explicitly framed as one ("imagine…"), and sparingly; a real study told well beats an invented scene every time.
- **Concrete before abstract.** Show the phenomenon, then name the mechanism. Define terms by apposition in the sentence itself — "the hippocampus, the brain's memory hub" — and still give the term its verified link: **name it, gloss it, link it.** Every linked term gets its gloss; a bare link with no apposition is scientific style, not popsci. This is the middle path between scientific style (link only) and ELI5 (rewrite entirely); the reader leaves knowing the real vocabulary.
- **Numbers become human-scale, without losing precision.** Lead with what the number means, keep the exact statistic in a parenthesis: "about one extra case for every 900 people vaccinated ([RR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_risk) 1.13, 95% CI 1.02–1.25)". Prefer absolute risks, frequencies ("one in eight"), and comparisons to familiar magnitudes over bare relative effects. A number the reader cannot interpret ("4 mm Hg") is glossed or cut, not printed raw.
- **The contrary evidence is the plot twist, not a footnote.** Popular science at its best treats the complication as the most interesting part. Give the turn its own crosshead, let it genuinely threaten the story so far, then resolve it the way the evidence resolves it — including "it doesn't, yet".
- **One metaphor family, carried through.** A single well-chosen image (a thermostat, a relay race, a leaky bucket) may recur and evolve across the piece. Mixed metaphors read as decoration; a sustained one is structure. If the headline plants an image, the body develops that image, not five others. It must never smuggle in a claim the sources don't make.
- **Rhythm and address.** Sentences vary: a long evidence sentence, then a short verdict. Second person and real questions are *expected devices, not grudging permissions*: shared bodily experience is written to "you" by default ("your brain senses the spike in your heart rate"), and every piece asks at least one genuine question at a real hinge — a feature with zero "you" and zero questions has kept the reader at arm's length. Spend the devices deliberately: direct address where the science touches the reader's own body or day, questions where the piece itself is turning; never as filler. First person plural ("we") only for the shared human situation, never for the analysis.
- **Let the papers speak.** A magazine gets its warmth from scientists talking; Grounded cannot interview and must never invent speech — but it may quote a source's *own written words*: under 15 words, inside quotation marks, attributed ("the trial's authors called the effect \"clinically meaningful\""), copied verbatim from the stored abstract or full text so the string can be checked against the evidence, with the normal citation on the sentence. Two or three such quotes per piece at most, each earning its place — a hedge, a verdict, or a phrase more vivid than paraphrase. Never quote from memory, never trim a hedge out of a quote, and never present written words as spoken ones.
- **The certainty survives the storytelling.** This is the rule that keeps popsci honest. Hedges stay hedged; a weak finding stays weak no matter how good the sentence feels; "scientists are still arguing about this" is a legitimate story beat, not a flaw to write around. Banned vocabulary: breakthrough, game-changer, revolutionary, holy grail, miracle, stunning, "scientists baffled".
- **Citations unchanged.** Every empirical sentence carries its `Author 2026` DOI link immediately before terminal punctuation, exactly as in every other style. This is the piece's quiet flex — magazine prose that can be checked line by line — so the links are worn lightly but never dropped.
- **Crossheads are evocative but honest.** A reader who skims only the headline and crossheads should come away with the true arc of the evidence, curiosity intact but never misled. The machinery ban in `writing-guide.md` applies to crossheads with full force: no crosshead may label its own function ("The turn: …", "The evidence", "Synthesis").
- Tables remain allowed where studies line up; the table is also where the numbers budget sends surplus precision. Introduce each from the running text in the piece's own voice, not with apparatus phrases ("are summarized in"). Figure captions follow the popsci register in `figure-captions.md`.
- The banned-filler list still applies: hooks are earned with concreteness, not with "In today's fast-paced world" or "Imagine a world where…" boilerplate.
- Word budgets match the scientific tiers: small 600–1,000, medium 1,500–2,500, large 3,500–6,000. Sources, angles, and search depth are unchanged from the chosen size.
- Popsci prints well too — offer the PDF export after delivering, as with scientific style.

## The voice: how a magazine feature actually sounds

The model is a staff writer at *The Atlantic* or *New Scientist* on a good day: warm, concrete, a little wry, never sloppy about the evidence. The test for every sentence is the **editor test**: would a magazine editor let this sentence through, or mark it "academese"? Structure alone does not make a feature — a chronological spine filled with journal-register sentences is still a review in costume, and that is the failure mode to fight.

**Do:**

- **Put people in the sentences.** This is the rule most often skipped, so it is now checkable: **every section** has at least one actor doing the work, and the lede and the turn must name who — a team, an institution, a year, a place — as the actor ("In 2019, a team in Helsinki began following two thousand night-shift nurses…"). Where names read awkwardly, "the trial investigators", "a second team", or "the researchers who pooled them" still beats an agentless "was estimated"; the reference point is a magazine feature, where someone acts in roughly every sixth sentence. Meta-analyses too: someone pooled those trials, and the pooling is an event with a motive ("one group gathered every trial that had ever tested the supplement — thirty-one of them — and asked a single question"). Every such detail comes from the paper's own record — its authors, stated affiliations, year — and the never-invent-colour rule applies with full force: when the record gives no place or team, write "researchers" and move on rather than inventing one. **Names and institutions travel only through the data path** — ledger → synthesis `actors` field → prose, always by copying, never by recall. The permitted actor forms, in rough order of preference:

  1. **Surname from the citation tag** — "Wharton and colleagues", "when Rubino's team split the volunteers". Always safe; standard magazine practice; the default.
  2. **"Researchers at <institution>"** — "researchers at McMaster University began following…" — only when that affiliation is stored on the author record in the ledger and copied into the synthesis's `actors` field. A welcome variation: institutions often read warmer than surnames. Never shorten, translate, or infer a city or country from an institution name unless that exact place is part of the stored string.
  3. **Given name + surname** — "Sean Wharton and colleagues" — only copied verbatim from the ledger's `authors_structured` via the synthesis. Never recall a first name, never guess one, never expand an initial: recalled names arrive as plausible soundalikes of the truth, which is fabrication in its most convincing form. The validator cross-checks every given-name-plus-surname pair against the ledger and fails the review on any mismatch.
  4. **Generic actors** — "one team", "the trial investigators", "researchers who pooled the studies" — always safe when the record offers nothing.

  If a detail is not in the ledger record, the sentence does without it — a generic actor telling a true story always beats a vivid actor telling an invented one. A piece in which every finding "was estimated" by an unnamed analysis has no one in it, and a story needs someone.
- **Use kitchen verbs.** The symptoms come back, the curve bends, the effect faded, the number held. The magazine's verbs are the ones people use about their own lives; save "attenuated", "exhibited", "demonstrated" for the journal.
- **Keep the machinery of science concrete.** Not "insomnia cohorts" but "people who couldn't sleep"; not "time courses" but "the curves"; not "the intervention arm" but "the people who got the real pill". The reader should never need the researcher's dialect to follow the story.
- **Write in scenes where the sources allow it.** "Half the volunteers got the real capsules. Nobody — not even the nurses handing them out — knew which half." Real details only, straight from the methods — but told as the moment it was.
- **Vary the music deliberately.** A long evidence sentence, then a short human one. A paragraph is allowed to end on four words. If three consecutive paragraphs close with the same cadence, the piece has flatlined — reshape one.

**Don't — each pair shows a shape to avoid and the shape to write:**

- ✗ "Some findings move in the reassuring direction." → ✓ "One finding is reassuring: the headaches faded within a week."
- ✗ "…the stress response no longer being hormonally buffered." → ✓ "…with the hormone gone, nothing buffers the stress response."
- ✗ "But the cohort blocks a deterministic reading." → ✓ "But it kills the certainty. Not everyone relapses."
- ✗ "Elevation of evening cortisol and suppression of melatonin release intensify." → ✓ "Evening cortisol climbs while melatonin falls."
- ✗ "The initial weeks are where the returning symptoms become measurable." → ✓ "Within weeks, the symptoms are back."
- ✗ "A meta-analysis of 24 randomized trials quantified relapse in insomnia cohorts." → ✓ "When one team pooled the twenty-four trials that had watched people stop the therapy, the relapse rate finally acquired a number." (Name the team or place only if the paper's record gives it.)

**Banned constructions in this style** (rewrite on sight): chains of two or more nominalizations ("the attenuation of the elevation of…"); gerund-subject pile-ups ("the rhythm no longer being entrained"); abstractions performing actions no abstraction can perform ("the literature constrains what can be promised"); and any sentence you cannot imagine reading aloud in a magazine podcast. The certainty rules do not soften with the register: the warmth lives in the verbs and the people, never in rounding a hedge up.
