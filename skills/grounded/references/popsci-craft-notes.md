# Craft behind the popular-science rules

Read when learning or revising `style-popsci.md`, not as extra required research
for every review. These are observations from published work, consulted on
5 September 2026. They inform editorial choices; they are not scientific
evidence for another article, templates to reproduce, or proof of universal
writing effectiveness.

## A puzzle gives explanation a purpose

Ciara Curtin's [Scientific American article about old glass](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/fact-fiction-glass-liquid/)
starts with a physical detail in cathedral windows and the explanation people
give for it. The article then moves into material structure and returns to the
windows. The opening supplies a question that the background helps answer.
The transferable technique is that return with new understanding, not the
article's exact language or every scientific simplification it makes.

## An investigation supplies forward movement

Ashley Balzer Vigil's [report on newly discovered octopuses](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/four-new-octopus-species-discovered-in-the-deep-sea/)
moves from a witnessed hatching to the work of distinguishing species.
Observable features become relevant because classification is the next problem.
The descriptive detail and scientific explanation belong to the same story.
Reporting provides the scenes; a literature-based author must not invent
equivalent eyewitness material.

## Background can enter when a result creates the need

Siddhant Pusdekar's [Quanta feature on sterilized soil](https://www.quantamagazine.org/the-dirt-that-refused-to-die-20260601/)
begins with an experimental outcome that would not go away. Attempts to explain
it lead into the chemistry. Alternative explanations arrive as part of that
investigation, so uncertainty advances the story instead of becoming a series
of appended cautions. The ending reconnects the investigation to a familiar
material. Its reported personal details require reporting access; the useful
general technique is observation, investigation and changed understanding.

## Research for the narrative, not only for the result

In [The Open Notebook's advice on explanatory narrative](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2014/11/11/finding-narrative/),
science journalists describe finding the questions, attempts and obstacles
behind results. The discussion also offers a history of changing ideas when a
single character cannot carry the piece. Grounded adapts this to source access:
look for documented experimental actions and intellectual development while
reading, and build the explanation from those details. Do not fabricate the
interviews, feelings or scenes available to an on-site reporter.

## Illustration has to teach as well as attract

Jen Christiansen's [Scientific American graphics essay](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/sa-visual/visualizing-science-illustration-and-beyond/)
distinguishes thematic editorial art from information graphics and discusses
how composition, simplification and annotation serve understanding. Her
examples show that engaging art and informative structure can coexist. Text
can orient a complex comparison; playful details can reinforce a relationship.
Grounded's numbered explanatory figures therefore need a visible information
job, not merely an attractive subject or a slogan. Labels and captions should
help reveal that job, rather than be forced out in pursuit of wordless art.

## Three regressions: abstract rules, over-correction, then thinness

In October 2026 a medium popsci review of infant night sleep passed every
evidence check, yet its reader found it convoluted and incomplete. It opened on
"the pattern in the question", which no reader of the article had seen. Most
findings were followed by a sentence about what they did "not establish".
Statistics arrived raw, and the text never showed how any study was done. The
September revision had replaced a concrete guide, built on worked ✗/✓ rewrites,
with abstract principles, and the writer took on the register of the
instructions.

The first repair over-corrected. It asked for the method of every study and
raised the word budget, and a 15-agent production line was built around a
condensed writer's brief, fact markers, a quiz and two editors. Blind tests on
held-out topics showed the result: accurate, method-rich articles of about 3,000
words that cited 30 to 40 sources and read as a tour of the literature. Cold
readers stumbled on sentences carrying four to nine numbers, on arithmetic shown
in the prose, on units nobody can picture and on paragraphs listing one study
after another. Over two topics the heavy line did no better than a single writer
who had the sources (it lost one blind comparison and narrowly won the other),
and it used about seven times as many agent calls. Three of its parts made
things worse:
- **The condensed brief.** A writer who saw only the brief lost the detail that
  makes a study vivid.
- **Long reader-question lists.** These pulled a short article toward side
  issues.
- **Revisions that tried to satisfy every reader.** These added hedges and
  calculations.

A third round then prized selectivity above everything. It cured the tour and
made the articles thin: one-line answers dropped into paragraphs they did not
belong to, a table of risk ratios in mid-story, and topics the answer rested on
left out. Readers called the result "assembled notes" and ranked it last on the
topic where the first repair had produced its best article.

One cause sat outside the writing rules. The audit's number check accepted a
figure only in the source's own form, so a writer who wrote "about 16 minutes"
for 15.8 was sent back to "−0.18 (95% confidence interval −16.1 to 15.8)". The
check now accepts marked rounding, a minus sign carried by a direction word,
units that convert exactly, plain frequencies and spelled numbers, and still
rejects wrong values, conflicting units and unmarked rounding.

What survived testing is in the current rules:
- One continuous story built on a handful of studies told as small stories, with
  the rest of the evidence in a sentence and everything the answer rests on
  covered.
- Tools for the reader: a yardstick, what a number is worth, how to read the
  evidence, why results differ.
- Plain numbers in the prose with their rounding marked, and the exact figures in
  a table after the ending.
- A short list of reader questions that steers a background search and the plan,
  and is never a checklist for the text.
- A writer with a clear desk and the stored texts.
- A deterministic readability profile that points at overloaded passages.
- Two cold readers whose notes are triaged before a single revision.

Blind comparisons in October 2026 used one model for every condition and four
readers per topic, so they show direction, not proof:
- **Rules before any repair:** ranked last in all eight rankings on two held-out
  topics.
- **Current rules against the first repair, fresh topic (stopping
  semaglutide):** preferred by four of four readers, one quiz item lower out of
  twelve, and judged the more faithful of the two.
- **Same comparison on seed oils**, where the first repair had written its best
  article: second in three of four rankings and first in one. Readers found the
  current article richer but said its back half turned into a catalogue.
- **Complete run on the infant-sleep question against the October original:**
  preferred by four of four readers, with "how the studies were done" rated 9
  against 5.

The weaknesses readers still named became rules: say a shared limitation once,
keep the back half an argument, make every table row a finding the story told,
and point to a figure from inside a sentence.

Two further complete runs showed how much one question varies from run to run:
on the infant-sleep question readers rated ease of reading 8 for one run and 6
for the next, though both were rated 8 to 9 on how the studies were done and
both beat the October original in every ranking. The heavier article's worst
sentences had a single origin. They were added by the cold-read revision, which
had tried to satisfy the sceptical test reader: an explanation of what an
interval is not, a counter-evidence trial with its access caveats, a stack of
limitations after one result. The cold-read step now asks that reader for errors
only, and the revision cuts and rewrites before it adds. Run on its own, the
rewritten step improved both articles it was tried on (preferred over the
unrevised text by four of four readers each). A first wording cut too freely and
removed the reasons behind the methods, which readers missed; the step now lists
what to keep. It lifts a heavy article; it does not supply the direct answer,
practical thread and reassurance that the best draft had from the start.

An August 2026 feature on blood pressure, written under the older concrete
guide, remains the reference for register: about 1,100 words, four anchor
studies, and crossheads such as "Then the dietitian goes home".

## What published features' paragraphs do

The repository's `evals/popsci-writing/popsci-feature-corpus.json` labels every
body paragraph of 24 publicly readable features (Quanta, The Conversation,
Science News, Yale E360, Hakai, Eos, Undark, Greater Good, SAPIENS; 2017–2026;
medicine, biology, physics, earth science, psychology and technology), consulted
4 October 2026. The labels came from one annotator with no second-rater check,
and the file stores no article text.
- **Openings:** 17 of 24 pieces open on a scene, and the nut graf arrives by a
  median paragraph 3.
- **Methods:** method paragraphs are short and paired with results. They make up
  a median 8.5% of paragraphs, almost always one at a time, and most are followed
  by a finding within two paragraphs.
- **Complications:** they appear about three times per article, rarely two in a
  row, and mostly after the midpoint.
- **Numbers:** a quarter of paragraphs carry a number.
- **Paragraph length:** the median paragraph is 56 words.
- **Practical paragraphs:** these grow towards the end.
- **Researcher-written versus journalist-written:** pieces by researchers quote
  people in 3% of paragraphs, against 36% for journalists, and give more space to
  methods and findings. Researcher pieces are the closer model for a
  literature-based writer who cannot interview anyone.

These distributions inform the style guide and the evaluation tools. They are
never quotas.

## Applied decisions

The style guide requires a sustained story but leaves its form open. Research
notes gather the material needed to tell it. Background follows the reader's
need; the ending delivers an understanding of the opening subject. Scientific
limits stay where they affect meaning, while verification logistics stay in
the apparatus. Figure planning starts with what becomes easier to understand,
then chooses the art. These decisions are assessed on complete unfamiliar
articles and actual images, not inferred from word counts or filled fields.
