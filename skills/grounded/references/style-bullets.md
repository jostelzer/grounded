# Bullet style

Use only when the user asks for bullets, a list or the compact structured format. Use the scientific reporting voice and shared evidence and editorial rules in `writing-guide.md`. Compression removes filler and redundancy while preserving the relationships between findings.

## Structure

```text
## <The question>

**TL;DR** — <The answer and its uncertainty in 1–3 citation-free sentences.>

### <A concise finding or question defining this section's contribution>
- <A complete sentence developing the point, with local citations for empirical claims.>
- <Evidence, explanation or a qualification that connects to the preceding point.>

### <The next part of the answer>
- …

**Scope and methods** — <The shared disclosure.>

**Sources**
<The generated source block, followed by receipts.>
```

Keep section bodies in bullets, with tables and figures where useful. Headings should let a reader follow the answer; a calibrated claim heading works when the section supports it, and a descriptive heading works when it better represents mixed evidence.

Each bullet develops a point rather than reproducing a ledger entry. Use complete, concise sentences and include the comparison, study design, estimate or qualification needed to interpret the finding. A bullet may explain how results relate; it need not introduce another study. Do not force all evidence into affirmative claims or repeat the heading in each item.

Order bullets so the comparison or reasoning develops. Group genuinely parallel findings and make differences in outcome or design explicit when material. Move dense comparable quantities to a table without separating an estimate from its necessary uncertainty. No fixed number count overrides meaning.

Use bold sparingly for decisive numbers or terms. Apply the shared reporting voice to captions and the conclusion. Length and research depth follow the selected size in `sizes.md`; bullet format does not reduce the evidence requirements or excuse disconnected writing.
