# Popular-science style

Use for the default review or an explicit popular-science, magazine or science-journalism request. Write the kind of feature a curious adult reads for pleasure in a good science magazine and finishes. Afterwards they should be able to tell a friend what is going on, how it works, how scientists found out, and how sure anyone can be. `popsci-pipeline.md` gives the working steps; this file says what the article must be. Apply the shared reporting voice in `writing-guide.md`.

The article is one continuous story, not a tour of the literature and not a list of answers. Its backbone is a handful of studies, each told as a small story the reader can picture; the rest of the evidence supports them in a sentence. Drafts fail readers in two opposite ways: some pack every study, statistic and caveat into dense prose, and others are easy and empty, a tidy list of averages. The cure for both is the same: tell fewer things properly, and spend the space on what makes the evidence mean something.

Two tests apply to every paragraph. **The editor test:** would a good magazine editor let it through, or mark it "academese"? **The read-aloud test:** could it be read on a science podcast without stumbling or rereading? A paragraph that is accurate but fails either test is not finished.

## What the reader must come away with

A feature is complete when someone who never saw the request, the sources or the plan can answer these from the article alone:

1. **What is going on?** The phenomenon, concretely: what it looks like, how common it is, at what scale.
2. **How does it work?** The mechanism, as deep as the sources support, as a picture the reader can hold before any technical name. If it is unknown or disputed, say what the candidate explanations are.
3. **How do we know?** For each study the answer leans on: who took part, what was actually done to or by them, compared with what, measured how and by whom, for how long. Methods are not paperwork for the scope note. For a lay reader they are the most interesting part of science, and the only way to judge a finding.
4. **How big is it?** In plain words and familiar units.
5. **What does it mean for the situation that prompted the question?** Including what the people in the studies actually did and what named guidelines say. Report it; do not advise (`writing-guide.md`).
6. **What is still unknown?** A specific gap and why it matters, not a reflex "more research is needed".

Size sets the depth of each answer, not whether it is answered. The article also covers every claim the synthesis Verdict rests on and every popular belief the question is really about, each at least in a sentence. If the sources cannot answer something, say so in a sentence.

## Find the angle, and keep its promise

The angle is the most interesting true thing the evidence says about the question, stated as a tension, a surprise or a turn: "Thirteen points under supervision, four in everyday life"; "An hour, with lessons or without". A neutral survey ("How much can lifestyle lower blood pressure?") is a topic, not an angle, and it produces a list. Look where results differ from what a reader would expect, where two kinds of evidence disagree, or where a measurement changes the story. Never invent a tension the sources do not contain.

An angle is a promise. If the headline asks why thirteen points become four, the article answers: the best-supported reading, how sure it is, and what would settle it. "The trials cannot isolate the reason" is where that answer starts, not where it ends. A reasoned reading of the evidence, labelled as a reading, is part of the reporter's job.

Choose a form that suits the evidence. A discovery story follows attempts and consequences; an explanatory mystery follows observations and competing explanations; a journey through a system follows a process across places or scales; a followed study tracks one investigation, with the wider literature arriving as context. These are options, not templates. Never invent a chronology, rivalry, surprise, motive or scene. Without documented personal history, tell the story of the phenomenon or the experiment itself.

## Shape of the feature

- **The article stands alone.** The reader never saw the request. Never refer to it: no "the pattern in the question", "as asked", "this review", "the studies described here". If the request describes a personal situation, turn it into a scene, plainly framed as a hypothetical: "Picture a night that runs three hours, then three more, then one and one."
- **Headline:** the question recast as a magazine title: concrete, curious and honest. It must not promise a payoff or a certainty the evidence cannot deliver.
- **Standfirst:** 1–3 italic, citation-free sentences in plain words: what the piece is about, why it is interesting, the gist of the answer.
- **Opening:** the article begins with prose, not a crosshead. Enter through one study told as a scene, a striking sourced observation or a clearly framed hypothetical. The first sentence must make sense to someone who has read only the headline. By the third paragraph the reader knows the question and the short answer. Do not hold the answer back; the story is in how we know and what it means.
- **Body:** a few substantial sections that follow one line of thought, each built around a study or an explanation and each leading to the next. Background enters when the story needs it. One section explains how the thing works. A crosshead over only a paragraph or two usually marks a fragment that belongs inside its neighbour. The later sections belong to the argument as much as the first: once each remaining topic gets a paragraph and a study of its own, the back half has become a catalogue. Fold a secondary outcome into the paragraph it bears on, in a sentence, and cut a finding that leads to nothing the reader can use.
- **No scattered answers.** The reader's questions shape the research and the plan; they are not a checklist to tick off in the text. A question earns its place where the story reaches it. Never drop a one-sentence answer into a paragraph it does not belong to, and never leave a sentence standing alone as a paragraph unless it is doing deliberate work.
- **What people actually do:** when the question comes from a practical situation, report what participants in the trials actually did, day to day, what a realistic change achieved, and what named guidelines say, attributed to them. Give the particulars: what a session, a serving or a routine consisted of. Gather them in one paragraph near the end, because readers look there for what the helpful programmes boil down to: "The practical thread running through the sessions was a calm, repeatable bedtime routine, attention to signs of tiredness and hunger, and quiet soothing when a baby woke." It is never the narrator's advice.
- **Ending:** return to the opening with what can now be explained, give the answer once more in a plain sentence, and end on a concrete implication or the one open question that matters. No summary list, no generalized caution. The prose ending comes before any table of figures.
- **The figures table comes last.** In a medium or large feature, put the exact figures behind the story in one compact table after the ending, under a plain crosshead such as "The numbers behind the story": the six or eight key results, what each compares, what it rests on (studies, people) and its range, in plain column names and about 200 words, with one study and one result per row and nothing packed into a cell. Readers who want to check can; readers who do not have already finished. Every row is a finding the story told, under the name the story gave the study, with the same counts and the same direction: a row the reader has never met, two studies sharing one name, or a figure that disagrees with the text costs more trust than the table earns. A small feature may leave it out. A comparison table that the story itself needs, with a few plain cells, may sit in the body.
- **Write to the target length** in `budgets.md`, give or take a tenth. A draft well under it has usually skipped the stories, yardsticks and concrete details that make a feature rewarding. Never go over the top of the range.
- **Apparatus:** end with the short scope and methods note, written for the same reader in three or four plain sentences: what kind of review this is, when and where the literature was searched, what was left out, and which key studies could be read only as summaries. No workflow words. It says how this review was made; it never replaces explaining how the studies were done.

Crossheads are short and say something. Someone who reads only the headline and crossheads should get the true arc: "An hour, with lessons or without", "Then the dietitian goes home". Never label a section by its function ("Background", "The evidence", "Limitations"), and make sure each section delivers what its crosshead promises.

## Tell studies as small stories

Researchers and participants are the actors. Show what they did, using only details the paper records: who, where, what was done, the comparison, the instrument, the duration, the year. Explain each piece of method in ordinary words at the point where it matters. "Randomized" becomes "assigned by lot"; "usual care" becomes what the comparison group actually received; an instrument is described by what it does.

Give the main studies room: a paragraph or two each, with the one or two details that make them easy to picture. A medium feature usually tells four to six studies this way, a small one two or three. Other evidence supports them in a sentence, with its design in a few words ("a review of 133 trials"), not a full account. Do not give every source its own sentence, and do not line studies up one per paragraph: each one enters because the story needs it.

Each pair below rewrites a sentence from a real Grounded draft, using only facts the draft already contained.

> ✗ In the EAT trial, exclusively breastfed three-month-old infants were randomized to early food introduction alongside breastfeeding or standard introduction around six months.
>
> ✓ The EAT trial took three-month-old babies who were fed only breast milk and split them into two groups by lot. One group started tasting solid foods straight away, alongside breastfeeding; the other waited until around six months, the standard timing.

> ✗ It randomized 246 first-time mothers and their infants to early sleep education or usual care, then measured the longest infant night sleep period at six and twelve weeks, principally using ankle-worn movement monitors.
>
> ✓ The researchers recruited 246 first-time mothers and assigned them by lot either to early lessons about infant sleep or to usual care. Most babies wore a movement monitor on the ankle, which recorded their longest night-time stretch at six weeks and again at twelve, so the main result did not rest on tired parents' memories.

Introduce researchers when their actions explain the investigation; do not restart every finding with an author name. Surnames from citation tags and accurate generic descriptions ("one team", "the trial investigators") are always safe. Given names and institutions must be copied from the ledger's structured author records through the synthesis `actors` field. Never infer an affiliation, expand an initial, or invent a scene, participant, motive or sensory detail. When two sources of the same kind appear, tell them apart by what they are ("a 2024 review of 31 studies"), not by "the review".

## Give the reader tools, not only results

Readers remember, and recommend, the article that leaves them able to reason about the subject. Work these into the story where the sources support them; they are part of the telling, not extra sections:

- **A yardstick.** Size the key numbers against something the reader already knows: a standard treatment, an everyday amount, a normal range. One good yardstick, set up early, makes every later number meaningful.
- **What a number is worth.** What a change in the measured quantity means for the outcome people care about, and where that translation comes from.
- **How to read the evidence, taught once.** When a design feature decides what the numbers mean (a comparison group that also improved, a measurement that flatters, who was counted), explain it once as a small puzzle: what would fool you without it?
- **Why results differ.** Readers always ask why. When two studies or two settings give different answers, lay out the two to four candidate reasons, each with the fact that makes it plausible, then say what the evidence can and cannot separate.
- **What happens next.** Whether the effect lasts, and what happens when the thing stops, if the sources say.
- **The detail that sticks.** A concrete particular a reader would repeat to a friend.

> ✓ One standard blood-pressure pill takes about 9 points off the top number. Against that yardstick, the supervised programme's 13 points is about a pill and a half, and the 4 points people managed on their own is less than half a pill.

> ✓ The group that changed nothing improved too, by about 3 points. People join trials when their readings are high, and high readings tend to drift back toward a person's usual level. That is why every figure here is the gap between two groups, not the fall in one.

> ✓ A ban took about 40 minutes of phone use out of the school day. The same pupils spent four to six hours a day on their phones in total.

> ✗ The two trials differed in several respects, and the reason for the gap remains unresolved.
>
> ✓ Why 13 points in one trial and 4 in the other? The trials differ in at least three ways that could matter. One brought people to supervised sessions three times a week; in the other they shopped and cooked for themselves. One compared the programme with doing nothing, the other with advice. And one ran four months, the other six. Nobody has tested which of these explains the gap, so "supervision works" is a reading of the evidence, not a result.

## Make numbers mean something

- **Every finding the story rests on gets its size, as a number the reader could repeat to a friend.** "Several times higher" or "a small benefit" is not a finding; "about four and a half times the rate" and "about a quarter of an hour more sleep" are.
- **Say it in the reader's terms.** Lead with what the number means, in everyday forms: "about a quarter of an hour", "one in eight", "roughly a third as large". Prefer absolute effects and frequencies, and give the starting level when a relative change would otherwise float free. Never derive an absolute risk from a relative effect without the baseline.
- **Mark every rounded figure** with "about", "roughly", "nearly" or "more than". The assertion audit accepts marked rounding and rejects a silently rounded number. Never rewrite a readable sentence into "measured in minutes, the difference was −0.18" to satisfy a check.
- **Use units people can picture.** Grams, not millimoles; what one "drink" or "session" contained. Use a labelled standard conversion when the sources allow it; otherwise explain the unit once in a few words.
- **Keep the statistics light.** A sentence carries one or two numbers. Intervals, ratios, p-values and standard deviations stay out of the sentences; when the uncertainty matters to the story, say what it means in plain words ("the data fit anything from about 16 minutes shorter to 16 minutes longer"). The exact figures go in the table at the end.
- **Do the arithmetic for the reader, off the page.** Give the result of a subtraction, not the working. One short label such as "by subtraction" is enough when a figure is the article's own.
- **Compare like with like, and say what each number counts.** Set a net difference against a net difference. When two figures seem to clash because they measure different things, say so in a clause or use only one.

> ✗ Measured in minutes, the programme difference was −0.18 (95% confidence interval −16.1 to 15.8); that result provides little support for a substantial benefit from this particular programme.
>
> ✓ The lessons made no detectable difference. On average, babies in the two groups had the same longest stretch, give or take a fraction of a minute. Allowing for chance, the data fit anything from about 16 minutes shorter to 16 minutes longer, so a large benefit from this programme is unlikely.

> ✗ Over an average of 4.7 years, strokes occurred at about 29 per 1,000 people per year with the substitute and about 34 with regular salt, a 14 percent lower rate (rate ratio 0.86; 95% confidence interval 0.77 to 0.96).
>
> ✓ Over nearly five years, the villages using the substitute had about a seventh fewer strokes: roughly 29 a year for every 1,000 people, against about 34.

> ✗ The plastic-positive group had a higher rate of heart attack, stroke or death. The estimated difference was several-fold, although its size was uncertain.
>
> ✓ Patients with plastic in their plaque had heart attacks, strokes or died at about four and a half times the rate of the others. The estimate is loose: the data fit anything from about twice the rate to ten times.

## Spend caveats where they change the meaning

Give the finding first, then the one limitation that most changes how far to trust it. Say concretely what could have gone wrong and in which direction. Build scope into the sentence ("in 246 first-time mothers") instead of adding a disclaimer.

> ✗ Sleep was a secondary outcome reported by parents who knew their allocation, leaving a possible reporting bias.
>
> ✓ The trial was designed mainly to answer a different question, and the sleep figures came from parents' own records. Because parents knew which group they were in, some of the difference may reflect what they expected to see.

State each limitation once, where it matters most; as a working limit, one sentence of limitation per section. When one limit applies to several studies (none of them measured the outcome the reader cares about), say so once, where it first matters, and rely on it afterwards. A refrain that returns in every section ("which leaves the longest stretch unanswered") reads as hedging, however true it is. Guard only against misreadings a reader is likely to make. Do not end paragraphs with what a finding "does not establish", "does not prove" or "cannot diagnose". General cautions, such as group averages not predicting an individual or associations not proving cause, belong in one considered place. Uncertainty that is interesting, such as two explanations competing or a surprising null result, is part of the story. Uncertainty repeated by reflex is noise.

> ✗ That observation makes frequent waking compatible with common developmental variation; it does not establish the cause of an individual infant's waking.
>
> ✓ In other words, a six-month-old who does not yet sleep six hours straight has plenty of company.

> ✗ The medicine estimate comes from separate drug trials, so it supplies a scale comparison.
>
> ✓ Keep in mind that the pill figure comes from other trials, in other people.

## Write plainly, and with some life

- **Concrete subjects, active verbs.** Babies, patients, cells and researchers do things. "Night sleep consolidates" becomes "babies start sleeping in longer blocks". Use the verbs people use about their own lives: the stretch grew, the effect faded, the number held.
- **One main idea per sentence, in paragraphs that develop a point.** Vary the length: after a long sentence that carries evidence, a short one can land the point. A sentence with two qualifications and a contrast becomes two or three sentences. A paragraph has a point and the sentences that earn it; a run of one- or two-sentence paragraphs reads like notes.
- **Explain before you name.** Describe the thing, then give the technical term with a short gloss in the same sentence; link the term to its verified Wikipedia article only when a reader would want to look it up. Gloss body parts, units and lab methods too. Once glossed, use the real term; do not coin a substitute the reader will never meet again. Keep the machinery of science concrete: "people who couldn't sleep", not "insomnia cohorts"; "the families who got the lessons", not "the intervention arm".
- **Name the thing.** Do not make "this", "that observation" or "these findings" the subject of a sentence when the referent sits two sentences back. Call a study by something the reader can picture ("the Toronto trial", "the camera study") and keep that name; an author's surname means nothing to this reader, and two studies never share a name.
- **Say what a thing is, not what it is not.** "The study counted alcohol content, rather than glasses or bottles" and "a comparison of scale, rather than a contest" are caveats in disguise, and a run of them sounds like a lawyer. Use a "rather than" or "not X but Y" sentence only for a misreading the reader is about to make.
- **Every sentence carries something.** Cut the sentence that only sounds like a conclusion: "An early fall and a maintained fall are separate achievements." If a paragraph needs a last line, make it a fact, a consequence or the question the next section answers.
- **Let the piece have a voice.** A real question where the story turns, "you" for shared experience ("sleep pressure builds the longer you are awake"), a dry aside, a crosshead with a turn of phrase. One well-chosen comparison that preserves the relationship it explains is worth more than five adjectives. These are tools, never a channel for advice, and none of them may carry a claim the sources do not make.
- **No commentary on the article's own construction.** Do not tell the reader that a figure "deliberately keeps" things separate. Say what the figure shows.

> ✗ The evidence therefore describes a changing system, rather than a fixed nightly schedule waiting to be corrected.
>
> ✓ Two systems decide when anyone sleeps. One is a body clock that learns the difference between day and night. The other is sleep pressure, which builds the longer you are awake and drains away during sleep. In the first months, both are still developing.

> ✗ In Figure 2, the age-related change and the randomized programme difference are deliberately kept separate.
>
> ✓ Figure 2 puts the two numbers side by side: about an hour gained between six and twelve weeks across both groups, and essentially nothing added by the lessons.

Quote a source's written words only when the wording adds something a paraphrase would lose: short, verbatim from stored text, attributed and cited, never presented as speech.

## Make the pictures part of the explanation

Place a figure where the story asks the reader to picture a relationship: a hidden structure, a sequence, an experimental contrast, a change of scale. Plan what becomes understandable from seeing it. An attractive scene with a reassuring slogan is not an explanatory figure. Apply the whole-figure deletion test in `figure-generation-contract.md`. Captions follow `figure-captions.md` in the same plain register: say what each axis or panel shows, then what to notice. Point to a figure from inside a sentence that says something ("the machine and the parent each miss something different, as the two scenes in Figure 2 show"); the pointer never gets a sentence or a paragraph of its own.

## Check it, then read it cold

Run `scripts/readability_profile.py` on the draft. It counts what tires readers (numbers per sentence, statistics in prose, very long or very choppy passages, source roll-calls, reflex caveats, sentences that define by negation, talk about the article itself) and lists the worst passages. Rewrite those passages; a flag is a question, not a verdict, and a topic that is about quantities will carry more numbers than one that is not.

Nobody can read their own draft cold. The cold readers in `popsci-pipeline.md` stand in for the audience. Their confusion is a writing failure, not a reader failure. Revise at the level of the problem: a missing mechanism or method means going back to the sources, a fragmented section means restructuring, an unclear sentence means rewriting it, not adding a gloss on top. After any revision, re-read each changed section as a whole: a revision must never leave an orphaned sentence, a crosshead that no longer fits or a paragraph that has lost its point.
