# Popular-science style

Use for the default review or an explicit popular-science, magazine or science-journalism request. Write the kind of feature a curious adult reads for pleasure in a good science magazine. When they finish, they should be able to tell a friend three things: what is going on, how scientists found out, and how sure anyone can be. Accuracy, a sustained story and easy reading are joint requirements. Apply the shared reporting voice in `writing-guide.md`.

Two tests apply to every paragraph. **The editor test:** would a good magazine editor let it through, or mark it "academese"? **The read-aloud test:** could it be read on a science podcast without stumbling or rereading? A paragraph that is accurate but fails either test is not finished.

## What the reader must come away with

A popsci article is complete when someone who has never seen the request, the sources or the plan can answer these questions from the article alone:

1. **What is going on?** The phenomenon, described concretely: what it looks like, how common it is, at what scale.
2. **How does it work?** The mechanism, as deep as the sources support. Give a concrete picture before the technical name. If the mechanism is unknown or disputed, say what the candidate explanations are.
3. **How do we know?** For each study the answer leans on: who took part, what was actually done to or by them, what they were compared with, what was measured and how (the instrument, and who recorded it), and for how long. Methods are not paperwork for the scope note. For a lay reader they are the most interesting part of science, and the only way to judge a finding.
4. **What was found, and how big is it?** Results in plain words and familiar units, then the statistics.
5. **What does it mean for the situation that prompted the question?** Connect the findings back to it. Report rather than advise (`writing-guide.md`); attribute any guidance to its source.
6. **What is still unknown, and why does it matter?** A specific gap and its consequence, not a reflex "more research is needed".

Size sets the depth of each answer, not whether it is answered. A small piece may handle a question in two sentences; a medium piece gives each anchor study a paragraph that shows how it was done. If the sources cannot answer one of these questions, say so in a sentence rather than skipping it.

## Gather the story while researching

While reading, collect the concrete material a story needs: a revealing observation, what an investigation actually did, a consequential comparison, a change in understanding. Read methods sections and earlier work for these details; an abstract's conclusion is seldom enough.

For each **anchor study** (one whose result carries part of the answer), note from the full text: who the participants were and where; what the intervention or exposure looked like in practice (who delivered it, when, how often, what exactly was done); the comparison group; how the outcome was measured and by whom; the duration; and one concrete detail that makes the study easy to picture. Record these in `notes.md` and add verbatim `quote:` lines from the methods to the relevant synthesis claims, so the audit can check every procedural detail the article describes. When a method is itself a point the article makes (for example, that parents' diaries miss quiet waking), give it its own synthesis claim.

In **Throughline**, write a short paragraph: the opening subject and question, the explanation that develops, the anchor studies that move it forward, and the understanding reached at the end. Then note where the article answers each of the six reader questions. A topic list, a catchy first paragraph followed by study summaries, or repeated reminders to be cautious is not a story.

Choose an angle and a form that suit the evidence. A discovery story follows attempts and consequences; an explanatory mystery follows observations and competing explanations; a journey through a system follows a process across places or scales; a followed study tracks one investigation, with the wider literature arriving as context. These are options, not templates. The subject may be a phenomenon, object, place, idea or investigation; a human protagonist is optional. Never invent a chronology, rivalry, surprise, motive or scene. Without documented personal history, tell the story of the phenomenon or the experiment itself.

## Shape of the feature

- **The article stands alone.** The reader never saw the request. Never refer to it in the article body: no "the pattern in the question", "the user", "as asked" or "this review". If the request describes a personal situation, turn it into a scene the reader can picture, plainly framed as a hypothetical: "Picture a night that runs three hours, then three more, then one and one."
- **Headline:** the question recast as a magazine title: concrete, curious and honest. It must not promise a payoff or certainty the evidence cannot deliver. Establish that two names refer to the same thing before switching between a brand and a generic name or other synonyms.
- **Standfirst:** 1–3 italic, citation-free sentences in plain words: what the article is about, why it is interesting, and the gist of the answer. It is an invitation, not a miniature abstract full of qualifications.
- **Opening:** enter through a specific, sourced observation or study, or a clearly framed hypothetical. By the second or third paragraph, tell the reader plainly what the piece will answer and give the short version of the answer. Do not hold the answer back for suspense; the story is in how we know and what it means.
- **Body:** a few substantial sections, each answering one reader question fully. Background enters when the story needs it. An anchor study is told as an event: what was done, what happened, what it means, and the one limitation that most changes how far to trust it. Other evidence then extends or challenges the point. A crosshead over only a paragraph or two usually marks a fragment that belongs inside its neighbour. Crossheads are plain and informative: someone skimming only the headline and crossheads should get the true arc of the evidence.
- **Ending:** deliver the promised understanding in plain words. Return to the opening subject with what can now be explained about it, and end on a concrete implication, observation or open question. A summary list, a generalized caution or an automatic call for more research is not a payoff.
- **Apparatus:** keep the shared scope/methods disclosure, generated Sources and receipts. The disclosure is about how this review was made; it never replaces explaining how the studies were done.

## Tell studies as events

Researchers and participants are the actors. Show what they did, using only details the paper records: sample size, setting, procedure, instrument, duration, year. Explain each piece of method in ordinary words at the point where it matters. "Randomized" becomes "assigned by lot"; "usual care" becomes what the comparison group actually received; an instrument is described by what it does.

Each pair below rewrites a sentence from a real Grounded draft, using only facts the draft already contained.

> ✗ In the EAT trial, exclusively breastfed three-month-old infants were randomized to early food introduction alongside breastfeeding or standard introduction around six months.
>
> ✓ The EAT trial took three-month-old babies who were fed only breast milk and split them into two groups by lot. One group started tasting solid foods straight away, alongside breastfeeding; the other waited until around six months, the standard timing.

> ✗ It randomized 246 first-time mothers and their infants to early sleep education or usual care, then measured the longest infant night sleep period at six and twelve weeks, principally using ankle-worn movement monitors.
>
> ✓ The researchers recruited 246 first-time mothers and assigned them by lot either to early lessons about infant sleep or to usual care. Most babies wore a movement monitor on the ankle, which recorded their longest night-time stretch at six weeks and again at twelve, so the main result did not rest on tired parents' memories.

A study can anchor several paragraphs, with other evidence entering to extend or challenge a specific point. Introduce researchers when their actions explain the investigation; do not restart every finding with an author name. Surnames from citation tags and accurate generic descriptions ("one team", "the trial investigators") are always safe. Given names and institutions must be copied from the ledger's structured author records through the synthesis `actors` field. Never infer an affiliation, expand an initial, or invent a scene, participant, motive or sensory detail.

## Make numbers mean something

Lead with what a number means in the reader's terms, then give the exact statistic in parentheses. Prefer absolute effects, frequencies ("one in eight") and familiar units; give everyday scale (grams rather than millimoles, what one "drink" or "session" contained) when the sources or a labelled standard conversion allow it. Never derive an absolute risk from a relative effect without the necessary baseline. Label derived arithmetic as such. Compare like with like: set a net difference against another net difference, never a group's raw change against another trial's difference from its control group. Keep one or two numbers per point in the prose; supporting precision goes into parentheses or a table, with units, denominators and uncertainty preserved.

Explain a confidence interval in plain words the first time one matters, then use the parenthetical form. A result that excludes a large effect is a finding worth stating plainly.

> ✗ Measured in minutes, the programme difference was −0.18 (95% confidence interval −16.1 to 15.8); that result provides little support for a substantial benefit from this particular programme.
>
> ✓ The lessons made no detectable difference. On average, babies in the two groups had the same longest stretch, give or take a fraction of a minute. Allowing for chance, the data fit anything from about 16 minutes shorter to 16 minutes longer, so a large benefit from this programme is unlikely.

> ✗ At six months, the early-introduction group averaged 16.6 more minutes of night sleep (95% confidence interval 7.8–25.4) and 1.74 rather than 2.01 wakings per night.
>
> ✓ By six months, the early starters slept about a quarter of an hour longer each night (16.6 minutes; 95% confidence interval 7.8–25.4) and woke slightly less often: 1.7 times a night instead of 2.0.

## Spend caveats where they change the meaning

Give the finding first, then the one limitation that most changes how far to trust it. Say concretely what could have gone wrong and in which direction. Build scope into the sentence ("in 246 first-time mothers") instead of adding a separate disclaimer.

> ✗ Sleep was a secondary outcome reported by parents who knew their allocation, leaving a possible reporting bias.
>
> ✓ The trial was designed mainly to answer a different question, and the sleep figures came from parents' own records. Because parents knew which group they were in, some of the difference may reflect what they expected to see.

State each limitation once, where it matters most. Guard only against misreadings a reader is likely to make. Do not end paragraphs with what a finding "does not establish", "does not prove" or "cannot diagnose" unless the misreading is a natural one. General cautions, such as group averages not predicting an individual or associations not proving cause, belong in one considered place, not in every paragraph. Uncertainty that is interesting, such as two explanations competing or a surprising null result, is part of the story. Uncertainty repeated by reflex is noise.

> ✗ That observation makes frequent waking compatible with common developmental variation; it does not establish the cause of an individual infant's waking.
>
> ✓ In other words, a six-month-old who does not yet sleep six hours straight has plenty of company.

## Write plainly

- **Concrete subjects, active verbs.** Babies, patients, cells and researchers do things. "Night sleep consolidates" becomes "babies start sleeping in longer blocks". Use the verbs people use about their own lives: the stretch grew, the effect faded, the number held.
- **One main idea per sentence.** Vary the length: after a long sentence that carries evidence, a short one can land the point. A sentence carrying two qualifications and a contrast should usually become two or three sentences.
- **Name the thing.** Do not make "this", "that observation" or "these findings" the subject of a sentence when the referent sits two sentences back or could be one of several things.
- **Explain before you name.** Describe the phenomenon, then give the technical term with a short gloss in the sentence itself and its verified link: name it, gloss it, link it. A bare link is scientific style. Keep the machinery of science concrete: "people who couldn't sleep", not "insomnia cohorts"; "the families who got the lessons", not "the intervention arm".
- **No commentary on the article's own construction.** Do not tell the reader that a figure "deliberately keeps" things separate or that a table "keeps unlike outcomes visible". Say what the figure or table shows.

> ✗ The evidence therefore describes a changing system, rather than a fixed nightly schedule waiting to be corrected.
>
> ✓ Two systems decide when anyone sleeps. One is a body clock that learns the difference between day and night. The other is sleep pressure, which builds the longer you are awake and drains away during sleep. In the first months, both are still developing.

> ✗ In Figure 2, the age-related change and the randomized programme difference are deliberately kept separate.
>
> ✓ Figure 2 puts the two numbers side by side: about an hour gained between six and twelve weeks across both groups, and essentially nothing added by the lessons.

Direct address and questions are welcome where they help. Use "you" for shared experience ("sleep pressure builds the longer you are awake") and a real question where the story turns. They are tools, not quotas, and never a channel for advice. An analogy must preserve the relationship it explains and stop before it misleads. Quote a source's written words only when the wording adds something a paraphrase would lose: short, verbatim from stored text, attributed and cited, never presented as speech.

## Make the pictures part of the explanation

Place a figure where the story asks the reader to picture a relationship: a hidden structure, a sequence, an experimental contrast, a change of scale. Plan what becomes understandable from seeing it. An attractive scene with a reassuring slogan is not an explanatory figure. Apply the whole-figure deletion test in `figure-generation-contract.md`. Captions follow `figure-captions.md` in the same plain register: say what each axis or panel shows, then what to notice ("Left: the difference the lessons made, essentially none. Right: growth in the longest stretch between six and twelve weeks, about an hour.").

## Read it cold, then revise

When the draft is complete, and before figures are produced, give it to a **cold reader**: a fresh agent with no access to the sources, notes, synthesis or plan. Ask it to read as a curious non-specialist and report: the main takeaway in its own words; every sentence it had to read twice; terms and numbers it could not interpret; which of the six reader questions it cannot answer from the article, especially how the key studies were done and how the phenomenon works; and where its attention flagged. A cold reader's confusion is a writing failure, not a reader failure.

Revise at the level of the problem. Missing methods or mechanism means returning to the sources and synthesis, not padding prose. Fragmented sections mean restructuring. Unclear sentences mean rewriting, not adding glosses on top. Record the cold reader's findings and your repairs in a few lines in the existing working notes. If no fresh agent is available, do the read yourself with every working file closed, and record that the read was not independent.

Then complete the shared editorial read in `writing-guide.md`. A source-supported but dull, fragmented or hard-to-follow feature is not ready.

The craft rationale and published examples are in `popsci-craft-notes.md`, for consultation when learning or revising this style. Borrow techniques, not distinctive phrasing, subjects or a publication's complete formula.
