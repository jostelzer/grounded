# Popular-science style

Use for the default review or an explicit popular-science, magazine or science-journalism request. Write for a curious adult: concrete, engaging and easy to follow, with the reporting voice and editorial acceptance check in `writing-guide.md`. The interest comes from understanding the question and the evidence.

## Shape of the feature

- **Headline:** recast the research question honestly. Avoid promises of certainty or a payoff the evidence cannot deliver. Establish equivalence before switching between a brand and generic name or other unfamiliar synonyms.
- **Standfirst:** 1–3 italic, citation-free sentences under the headline, stating the question and the shape of the answer. It replaces the scientific abstract.
- **Opening:** establish a concrete point of entry and develop it into the larger question. A real study's setup, a verified observation or a clearly described phenomenon can work. Include only context that helps the reader understand what follows.
- **Body:** use informative crossheads and connected paragraphs. Follow the questions needed to understand the answer. A chronology or an investigation can supply the structure when supported; neither is compulsory. Put contrary or null findings beside the conclusion they qualify, with space proportional to their importance.
- **Ending:** complete the question raised at the opening and state the remaining uncertainty. A return to an earlier detail is useful only when it deepens the answer; no mandatory callback, plot twist or future-study prescription.
- **Apparatus:** retain the shared scope/methods disclosure, generated Sources and receipts. Word budgets and research depth follow the selected size.

## Explain through concrete evidence

Develop the phenomenon before naming an unfamiliar mechanism. Name, briefly gloss and link necessary specialist terms at first use. Explain what a method lets researchers distinguish when that matters to the finding; omit procedural detail that does not help the answer.

A study can anchor several paragraphs, with other evidence entering to extend or challenge a specific point. Introduce researchers when their actions or identity explain the investigation, rather than restarting every finding with an author name. There is no actor quota. Surnames from citation tags and accurate generic descriptions are safe; given names and institutions must be copied from the ledger's structured author records through the synthesis `actors` field. Never infer affiliations, expand initials or invent scenes, participants, motives or sensory details.

Choose the numbers that carry the comparison and explain their scale. Prefer absolute effects or frequencies when available and appropriate; never derive an absolute risk from a relative effect without the necessary baseline. Use parentheses or a table for supporting precision, preserving units, denominators and uncertainty. Numeric density follows what the reader needs to understand, not a fixed count per sentence or section. Do not obscure a material difference merely to sound conversational.

Use familiar verbs and natural sentence lengths. Questions, analogies and quotations are optional; keep them only when they do explanatory work. Direct personal address is unnecessary. An analogy must preserve the relationship it explains and stop where it becomes misleading, without a required recurrence or ceremonial retirement.

Quote a source only when its wording contributes something the paraphrase would lose. Use short, verbatim-checkable excerpts from stored text, retain qualifications, attribute them and cite the claim. Written words must never be presented as an interview or remembered speech. All empirical details, including experiences, retain the shared citation and source-support requirements.

Figures and captions follow `figure-captions.md`. Describe what the image shows in the same accessible reporting voice, with its material limits; avoid instructions to the reader or decorative metaphors that make a schematic sound causal.
